\chapter{Introduction and Strong-Field Context}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Sagittarius A*: a dual strong-field laboratory}
\label{sec:duallab}

The compact radio source \sgra{} at the dynamical centre of the Milky Way is
the nearest supermassive black hole and the only one for which two
independent strong-field probes are available at once: the resolved orbits
of the S-star cluster within a few thousand gravitational radii
\citep{Gillessen2017,Gravity2018,Gravity2020,Gravity2022}, and the
horizon-scale image of its photon ring obtained by the Event Horizon
Telescope \citep{EHT2022}. Together they anchor the mass
($4.297\pm0.012\times10^{6}\,\msun$), the distance ($8277\pm9$~pc), and the
angular size of the emission ring ($51.8\pm2.3\,\muas$), making \sgra{} the
cleanest laboratory for testing general relativity (GR) in the
strong-field, low-velocity regime.

The case that the central object is a black hole was assembled over
five decades. The compact radio source itself was found in 1974 (Balick
\& Brown, who named it); infrared proper-motion monitoring of the
innermost stars began in the early 1990s with the Garching and UCLA
groups, and by the early 2000s individual stars — S2 above all — had
been watched through enough of an orbit to make the enclosed mass and
its compactness inescapable: several million solar masses inside a
pericentre of $\sim$120~au excludes every cluster of dark stellar
remnants on stability grounds, and the 2018--2020 relativistic
measurements closed the remaining loopholes at the orbital scale. That
programme was recognised with the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics (Genzel
and Ghez, with Penrose for the theoretical side); the 2022 EHT image
then carried the evidence from $\sim$$10^3\,R_S$ down to the photon
shell itself. This report takes the black-hole interpretation as its
working hypothesis and asks the quantitative next question: does one
Kerr metric, with one $(M, R_0, \astar)$, account for \emph{all} of it
at once?

A geometric accident makes \sgra{} uniquely suitable: it subtends the
largest angular gravitational radius of any black hole on the sky.
Stellar-mass holes are millions of times closer per unit mass but
billions of times smaller; the giant of M87 is fifteen hundred times
more massive but two thousand times farther. At
$\theta_g = \UasPerM\,\muas$, \sgra{} narrowly beats M87* and dwarfs
everything else — which is why the only two horizon-scale images in
existence are of precisely these two objects, and why the S-star
laboratory has no analogue anywhere else in the sky.

\section{The no-hair theorem as a falsifiable target}
\label{sec:nohairintro}

The no-hair theorem states that the exterior spacetime of an astrophysical
black hole is fully specified by its mass $M$ and spin $a$; in particular
the mass quadrupole moment is locked to $Q = -M a^2$. Prospects for testing
this with Galactic-centre stars were laid out by \citet{Will2008}, and with
horizon-scale imaging by \citet{Psaltis2016}. Both programmes require a
forward model that is exact in the strong field: post-Newtonian expansions
degrade at the S2 pericentre ($r_{\rm peri} \approx 1470\,R_S$), and the
photon ring is intrinsically a property of exact null geodesics
\citep{Bardeen1973,Johnson2020}.

It helps to place the two probes on the map of gravity experiments,
whose axes are the potential $\epsilon = GM/rc^2$ and the speed $v/c$.
Solar-system tests live at $\epsilon \lesssim 10^{-6}$; binary-pulsar
timing reaches $\epsilon \sim 10^{-6}$ orbitally but involves strongly
self-gravitating sources; gravitational-wave mergers probe
$\epsilon \sim 0.1$ dynamically for seconds. The Galactic-centre
programme occupies a distinct corner: S2 at pericentre samples
$\epsilon \approx 3.5\times10^{-4}$, $v/c \approx 0.026$ — a
\emph{stationary} strong-ish field traversed slowly, ideal for
precision spectroscopy of the metric itself — while the photon ring
samples $\epsilon \sim 0.1$--$0.5$ with photons, the static
strong-field limit. No other object offers both rungs simultaneously,
around the same mass, with both anchored to the same dynamical
calibration; that dual anchoring is what this report's single-code
approach is built to exploit.

\section{This programme}
\label{sec:programme}

This report describes such a forward model, built from first principles and
validated at every layer. The simulator integrates the Carter-separated
geodesic equations \citep{Carter1968} in Kerr spacetime, renders the
accretion flow with the standard relativistic thin-disk model
\citep{NovikovThorne1973,PageThorne1974}, fits real astrometric and
spectroscopic data for the star S2, and embeds a free-quadrupole
Hartle--Thorne metric \citep{HartleThorne1968,GlampedakisBabak2006} to
constrain departures from Kerr. The same geodesic core runs on the GPU for
interactive exploration, and every number quoted here is regenerated from
the code by a single reproduction target.

\section{Lineage of the imaging problem}
\label{sec:lineage}

The lineage of the imaging problem runs from \citet{Luminet1979}, who first
computed the appearance of a thin disk around a Schwarzschild hole, to the
modern gravity renderers of \citet{James2015}; our CPU reference tracer
follows the same backward ray-tracing logic but is held to numerical
standards set by the analytic theory (Chapter~\ref{ch:numerics}).

The theoretical apparatus this simulator rests on was assembled over
half a century. \citet{Kerr1963} found the spinning vacuum solution;
\citet{BoyerLindquist1967} gave it the coordinates in which it is most
usable, and in which the horizon structure is transparent.
\citet{Carter1968} discovered the fourth constant of motion that renders
the geodesics integrable — without it, no machine-precision ray tracer
would be possible — and \citet{BardeenPressTeukolsky1972} worked out the
circular-orbit structure (ISCO, photon orbits, binding energy) in the
closed forms this code implements verbatim. \citet{Bardeen1973} computed
the analytic shadow boundary, the ``critical curve'' against which our
Kerr renders are validated to machine precision.

The imaging problem then developed in parallel with the means to solve
it. \citet{CunninghamBardeen1973} traced the appearance of a star
orbiting an extreme-Kerr hole; \citet{Luminet1979} produced the first
image of a thin accretion disk around a Schwarzschild hole, complete
with the lensed secondary image that gives every black-hole picture its
characteristic halo; and four decades later \citet{James2015} brought
the same backward ray-tracing to cinema audiences through
\emph{Interstellar}'s Gargantua. Our CPU reference tracer follows this
lineage but is held to the numerical standards of the analytic theory
(Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}) rather than to visual plausibility.

The observational payoff arrived in the last decade. The GRAVITY
Collaboration detected the gravitational redshift of S2 at its 2018
pericentre \citep{Gravity2018} — confirmed independently by
\citet{Do2019} — measured the Schwarzschild precession of its orbit
\citep{Gravity2020}, and combined multiple stellar orbits into the most
precise mass and distance to date \citep{Gravity2022}. In the same
window the Event Horizon Telescope resolved the horizon-scale emission
ring of \sgra{} \citep{EHT2022}, whose diameter is set by the photon
shell of Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}. This report's forward model reproduces
findings from both of these observational programmes from one geodesic
core.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The specific contributions of this programme are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a turning-point-free second-order Mino-time integration of the Carter
equations with per-step constraint projection, which makes traced shadow
boundaries independent of integrator tolerance
(Chapter~\ref{ch:numerics});
\item machine-precision validation of the traced Schwarzschild shadow
against Synge's formula and of the Kerr shadow against the analytic
critical curve (Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow});
\item a 13-parameter full-geodesic fit to the public S2 data that
recovers the GRAVITY mass and distance and reproduces both published
relativistic effects with no tunable amplitudes
(Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting});
\item a quantified no-hair constraint from S2 alone, with a covariance
forecast for continued monitoring (Chapter~\ref{ch:nohair});
\item a validated double-precision GPU implementation of the same geodesic
core (Chapter~\ref{ch:gpu}); and
\item a single-command reproduction pipeline in which every number in
this report is regenerated from source (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Reader's guide}
\label{sec:readersguide}

The report is organised in four parts so that readers with different
backgrounds can enter at different depths.

\emph{Part~I — Foundations for the non-specialist} assumes no physics
background. It explains, in plain language and with almost no equations,
every concept the later chapters rely on: black holes and horizons, curved
spacetime, spin and the shadow, the accretion disk and its colours, stellar
orbits, and the statistical toolkit. Each primer chapter ends with a short
list of the terms just introduced and a pointer to the chapter where the
full treatment lives. Readers comfortable with general relativity can skip
Part~I entirely.

\emph{Part~II — Theory and Methods} derives, from first principles, every
formula the simulator implements: the Kerr geometry and its characteristic
radii (Chapter~\ref{ch:kerr}), the Carter-separated geodesics and their
second-order Mino-time form (Chapter~\ref{ch:geodesics}), and the numerical
integration machinery with its error analysis (Chapter~\ref{ch:numerics}).
Each derivation cites the source function that implements it.

\emph{Part~III — Results} presents the science: shadow and critical-curve
metrology (Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}), the relativistic thin disk
(Chapter~\ref{ch:disk}), photon-ring substructure (Chapter~\ref{ch:ring}),
the S-star forward model (Chapter~\ref{ch:sstar}), the S2 fit and its
statistics (Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}), the wider S-star cluster
(Chapter~\ref{ch:cluster}), and the no-hair constraint
(Chapter~\ref{ch:nohair}).

\emph{Part~IV — Engineering, validation, and closing} documents the GPU
implementation (Chapter~\ref{ch:gpu}), the \sgra{} calibration
(Chapter~\ref{ch:calib}), the interactive instrument
(Chapter~\ref{ch:instrument}), the development history and complete test
suite (Chapter~\ref{ch:devhistory}), and closes with discussion and
conclusions. The appendices carry extended algebra, algorithm pseudocode,
number provenance, the reproduction pipeline, and a glossary.

A reader who wants only the headline science should read
Chapters~\ref{ch:shadow}, \ref{ch:fitting} and \ref{ch:nohair}, plus the
calibration of Chapter~\ref{ch:calib}. A reader evaluating the numerics
should read Chapters~\ref{ch:numerics} and \ref{ch:devhistory}. Every
quantitative statement that is not a cited literature value is typeset from
a generated macro and is reproducible by \texttt{make reproduce}
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
