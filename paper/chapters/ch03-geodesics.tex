\chapter{Geodesics and the Carter Constant}
\label{ch:geodesics}

The simulator never integrates the raw geodesic equation
$\ddot x^\mu = -\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^\alpha \dot x^\beta$ for
photons; it integrates Carter's separated form, which is both faster and
— through its first integrals — self-diagnosing. This chapter derives
that form from the Hamilton--Jacobi equation, classifies the radial
motion by the roots of the resulting quartic, and develops the
second-order Mino-time formulation that the integrator of
Chapter~\ref{ch:numerics} actually solves. The implementing functions are
\texttt{radial\_potential[\_deriv]}, \texttt{polar\_potential[\_deriv]},
\texttt{null\_geodesic\_rhs} and \texttt{make\_null\_ray} in
\texttt{physics/src/null\_geodesics.cpp}, and \texttt{solve\_ut},
\texttt{carter\_q} in \texttt{timelike\_geodesics.cpp}.

\section{Hamilton--Jacobi separation}
\label{sec:hj}

Geodesics extremise proper length; equivalently they solve the
Hamilton--Jacobi equation for the action $S(x^\mu)$,
\begin{equation}
g^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\mu S\,\partial_\nu S = -\mu^2,
\label{eq:hje}
\end{equation}
with $\mu$ the rest mass ($\mu = 0$ for photons, and we normalise
$\mu = 1$ for stars). Stationarity and axisymmetry
(Section~\ref{sec:killing}) let us peel off the cyclic coordinates,
\begin{equation}
S = -E\,t + L_z\,\phi + S_r(r) + S_\theta(\theta),
\label{eq:ansatz}
\end{equation}
and \citet{Carter1968} discovered that with the inverse metric
\eqref{eq:ginv} the remainder separates completely. The substitution
deserves to be seen once in full. With $\partial_t S = -E$,
$\partial_\phi S = L_z$, the contraction \eqref{eq:hje} reads
\begin{equation}
\frac{-A E^2 + 4Mar\,E L_z
+ \dfrac{\Delta - a^2\sin^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta}\,L_z^2}{\Sigma\Delta}
+ \frac{\Delta\,S_r'^2 + S_\theta'^2}{\Sigma} = -\mu^2 ,
\label{eq:hjraw}
\end{equation}
where every coefficient is an entry of \eqref{eq:ginv}. The numerator of
the first term looks hopelessly mixed — $A$ and the $\sin^2\theta$
denominator couple $r$ to $\theta$ — but it regroups exactly:
using $A = (r^2+a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta\sin^2\theta$ and
$r^2 + a^2 - \Delta = 2Mr$ one verifies term by term that
\begin{equation}
\frac{-A E^2 + 4Mar\,EL_z + \bigl(\Delta/\sin^2\theta -
a^2\bigr)L_z^2}{\Delta}
= -\frac{P(r)^2}{\Delta}
+ \left(aE\sin\theta - \frac{L_z}{\sin\theta}\right)^2 ,
\label{eq:regroup}
\end{equation}
with $P(r)$ as defined below — the entire $t$--$\phi$ sector splits
into a pure-$r$ piece and a pure-$\theta$ piece, with no remainder.
This is the miracle of Kerr (the hidden Killing tensor of
Section~\ref{sec:killing} wearing coordinates); it is \emph{not}
generic, and its failure for the Hartle--Thorne metric is why the
no-hair machinery of Chapter~\ref{ch:nohair} must integrate
Christoffel symbols instead. Multiplying \eqref{eq:hjraw} through by
$\Sigma = r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta$ and applying \eqref{eq:regroup}, the
mixed terms assemble into
\begin{equation}
-\frac{1}{\Delta}\bigl[E(r^2+a^2) - a L_z\bigr]^2
+ \Delta\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}S_r}{\mathrm{d}r}\right)^2
+ \mu^2 r^2
= -\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}S_\theta}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\right)^2
- \left(aE\sin\theta - \frac{L_z}{\sin\theta}\right)^2
- \mu^2 a^2\cos^2\theta .
\label{eq:separated}
\end{equation}
The left side depends only on $r$, the right only on $\theta$; both must
equal a constant. Writing that constant as
$-\bigl[Q + (L_z - aE)^2\bigr]$ — the shift by $(L_z-aE)^2$ is a
convention that makes $Q$ vanish for equatorial orbits — and defining
\begin{equation}
P(r) = E(r^2+a^2) - a L_z,
\label{eq:pofr}
\end{equation}
the two sides become
\begin{align}
\Delta^2\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}S_r}{\mathrm{d}r}\right)^2
&= R(r) \equiv P(r)^2
- \Delta\bigl[\mu^2 r^2 + (L_z-aE)^2 + Q\bigr],
\label{eq:rpotgen}\\
\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}S_\theta}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\right)^2
&= \Theta(\theta) \equiv Q - \cos^2\theta\left[a^2(\mu^2 - E^2) +
\frac{L_z^2}{\sin^2\theta}\right].
\label{eq:thetapotgen}
\end{align}
$Q$ is the \emph{Carter constant}, the invariant associated with the
hidden Killing tensor of Section~\ref{sec:killing}; loosely, it measures
how far the orbit ranges in latitude ($\Theta \geq 0$ requires
$Q \geq 0$ for any orbit crossing the equator, with $Q = 0$ exactly for
equatorial motion). With four constants $(E, L_z, Q, \mu)$ for four
momentum components the system is Liouville-integrable: no chaos, every
photon path reducible to quadratures — the property that underwrites
both the machine-precision metrology of Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow} and the
closed-form checks used throughout the test suite.

Since $p_\mu = \partial_\mu S$, equations
\eqref{eq:rpotgen}--\eqref{eq:thetapotgen} are first-order equations of
motion,
\begin{equation}
\Sigma\,\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = \pm\sqrt{R(r)},
\qquad
\Sigma\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} =
\pm\sqrt{\Theta(\theta)},
\label{eq:firstorder}
\end{equation}
while raising $p_t$ and $p_\phi$ with \eqref{eq:ginv} gives the
longitudinal equations
\begin{align}
\Sigma\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} &=
  -\Bigl(aE - \frac{L_z}{\sin^2\theta}\Bigr) + \frac{a}{\Delta}P(r),
&\Sigma\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} &=
  -a(aE\sin^2\theta - L_z) + \frac{(r^2+a^2)}{\Delta}P(r).
\label{eq:tphi}
\end{align}
For photons ($\mu = 0$) these are the four equations implemented in
\texttt{null\_geodesic\_rhs}; the potentials and their radial/polar
derivatives are \texttt{radial\_potential[\_deriv]} and
\texttt{polar\_potential[\_deriv]}.

\subsection{Photons carry only two numbers: $\xi$ and $\eta$}
\label{sec:xieta}

A null geodesic is unchanged by rescaling its affine parameter, so for
$\mu = 0$ the three constants $(E, L_z, Q)$ act only through the two
ratios
\begin{equation}
\xi = \frac{L_z}{E}, \qquad \eta = \frac{Q}{E^2},
\label{eq:xieta}
\end{equation}
Bardeen's impact parameters. Dividing \eqref{eq:rpotgen} by $E^2$ and
expanding puts the radial potential in the form used for every
shadow-boundary computation in this report:
\begin{equation}
\frac{R(r)}{E^2} = r^4 + (a^2 - \xi^2 - \eta)\,r^2
+ 2M\bigl[\eta + (\xi - a)^2\bigr]\,r - a^2\eta .
\label{eq:bardeenquartic}
\end{equation}
Three of its structural properties do real work later. The cubic term
is absent — the sum of the four roots vanishes, so roots come in
balanced constellations. The coefficient of $r$ is non-negative, and at
$r \to 0^+$ the quartic approaches $-a^2\eta$: for $\eta > 0$ the
potential is negative just outside the ring singularity, so
equator-crossing photons can never reach $r = 0$. And at large $r$ the
quartic grows as $r^4$ regardless of aim — every photon is free at
infinity, which is what makes backward ray tracing from a distant
camera well-posed. For the camera itself the pair $(\xi, \eta)$ maps
affinely onto detector coordinates (Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}), so
\eqref{eq:bardeenquartic} really is ``the optics of the Kerr field''
in a single polynomial.

\subsection{Polar motion: the $u = \cos^2\theta$ quadratic}
\label{sec:polar}

The polar potential becomes elementary under $u = \cos^2\theta$. For
photons, $\Theta/E^2 = \eta + a^2u - \xi^2 u/(1-u)$; clearing the
$(1-u)$ denominator, the turning points in $u$ solve the quadratic
\begin{equation}
a^2 u^2 - \left(a^2 - \xi^2 - \eta\right)u - \eta = 0 ,
\label{eq:polarquad}
\end{equation}
whose two roots $u_\pm$ classify all latitudinal behaviour
\citep{Chandrasekhar1983, GrallaLupsasca2020}:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\eta > 0$: the product of roots $-\eta/a^2$ is negative, so
exactly one root $u_+$ lies in $(0, 1)$. The orbit oscillates
symmetrically about the equator between $\pm\theta_{\max}$,
$\cos^2\theta_{\max} = u_+$, crossing the equatorial plane each
half-cycle — the ``ordinary'' photons, and the only kind that can
image an equatorial disk repeatedly (the sub-ring count of
Chapter~\ref{ch:ring} is precisely the number of these crossings).
\item $\eta = 0$: $u = 0$ is a root — motion confined to the
equatorial plane, the case reduced to the cubic of
Section~\ref{sec:photonorbit}.
\item $\eta < 0$: both roots must be positive for motion to exist at
all; the orbit is \emph{vortical}, circulating around the spin axis
without ever crossing the equator. Such photons exist only for
$a \neq 0$ and only in a thin sliver of parameter space; they
contribute nothing to disk images but are traced correctly by the same
code path, and the polar-axis clamp of Section~\ref{sec:mino} is what
keeps their near-axis passages finite.
\end{itemize}
For timelike orbits the same substitution carries the extra term
$a^2(1 - E^2)u$; for the bound stellar orbits of Part~III, with
$E < 1$, the effect is a slight stiffening of the polar oscillation.
The S2 model exploits none of this structure directly — its orbit is
planar in the relevant sense — but \texttt{polar\_potential} evaluates
the general form, and the conserved-$Q$ diagnostic would flag any
latitudinal drift immediately.

\section{Turning points and orbit classification}
\label{sec:turningpoints}

$R(r)$ is a quartic in $r$ (for photons: leading term $E^2 r^4$), and
motion is allowed only where $R \geq 0$; the roots of $R$ are the radial
turning points, and their pattern classifies every orbit
(Fig.~\ref{fig:photonpot}):

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_photon_potential.pdf}
\caption{The radial potential $R(r)/E^2$ for equatorial photons at
impact parameters below, at, and above the critical value
(\texttt{physics\_tables} dump of \texttt{radial\_potential}). Left,
Schwarzschild: at $b = \sqrt{27}\,M$ the potential develops its double
root at $r = 3M$. Right, Kerr $\astar = 0.94$: the prograde
(low-$|b|$) and retrograde branches behave asymmetrically, and the
retrograde critical curve sits much farther out.}
\label{fig:photonpot}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{No positive root along the path} — the ray plunges from
infinity to the horizon: \emph{capture}. These pixels form the shadow.
\item \emph{A simple root at $r_{\rm turn}$} — the ray comes in, turns,
and returns to infinity: \emph{deflection}, from the gentle bending of
starlight up to arbitrarily many windings as the root is approached.
\item \emph{A double root at $\tilde r$} ($R = R' = 0$) — the marginal
case: an unstable circular (or, off the equator, spherical) photon
orbit. The set of $(E, L_z, Q)$ with double roots is a one-parameter
family — the \emph{photon shell} — whose image on the observer's sky is
the critical curve of Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}.
\item For timelike orbits the $\mu^2 r^2$ term turns the far field
around ($R \to (E^2 - 1)r^4/r^2\dots$): bound orbits have \emph{two}
simple roots $r_{\rm peri} \le r \le r_{\rm apo}$, the case exploited by
the Darwin-element construction of Chapter~\ref{ch:sstar}.
\end{itemize}

The instability of the double-root orbits is quantified by the curvature
of the potential there, $\tfrac12 R''(\tilde r)$; this single number
propagates all the way to the observable demagnification of the photon
sub-rings, via the Lyapunov exponents computed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}.

Root-counting sharpens the picture. Since \eqref{eq:bardeenquartic} has
no cubic term, its four roots sum to zero; for capture orbits all real
roots (if any) lie behind the horizon, for scattering orbits a
conjugate pair has moved out past $r_+$. As $(\xi, \eta)$ crosses the
critical curve, two real roots merge and annihilate — a fold
catastrophe, which is why observables vary \emph{smoothly} on either
side of the shadow edge but with a square-root cusp exactly at it. The
numerical consequence is practical: near-critical rays spend
arbitrarily long near the double root (logarithmic divergence of the
winding number), and it is precisely there that the adaptive stepping
of Chapter~\ref{ch:numerics} pays for itself, collapsing the step size
around the photon-shell passage and re-expanding after
(Fig.~\ref{fig:stepsize}).

\section{Mino time and the second-order formulation}
\label{sec:mino}

Two features make the first-order system \eqref{eq:firstorder} hostile
to naive numerics. The $\pm$ signs flip at every turning point, requiring
explicit bookkeeping; worse, at a turning point the right-hand side
$\sqrt{R}$ has infinite slope in $r$ ($\mathrm{d}\sqrt R/\mathrm{d}r \to
\infty$ as $R \to 0$), and an integrator stepping the truncated system
sees $\dot r = 0$ as a fixed point and stalls.

Both problems dissolve under two transformations. First, adopt the time
parameter of \citet{Mino2003}: define $\tau$ by
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{d}\lambda = \Sigma\,\mathrm{d}\tau,
\label{eq:minotime}
\end{equation}
which strips the common $\Sigma$ factor from all four equations — the
radial and polar sectors then evolve \emph{independently} of each other,
$(\mathrm{d}r/\mathrm{d}\tau)^2 = R(r)$,
$(\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}\tau)^2 = \Theta(\theta)$. Second,
differentiate once more with respect to $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
2\,\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}\tau}
   \frac{\mathrm{d}^2r}{\mathrm{d}\tau^2}
 = R'(r)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}\tau}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 r}{\mathrm{d}\tau^2} = \tfrac{1}{2}R'(r),
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 \theta}{\mathrm{d}\tau^2} =
\tfrac{1}{2}\Theta'(\theta).
\label{eq:secondorder}
\end{equation}
(The division by $\mathrm{d}r/\mathrm{d}\tau$ is legitimate except at
isolated turning points, where \eqref{eq:secondorder} holds by
continuity.) In this form a turning point is an ordinary smooth
extremum: the ``acceleration'' $\tfrac12 R'$ simply reverses the motion,
no square root of a near-zero quantity is ever taken, and no sign flag
exists to mis-set. The state vector the integrator carries is
$y = (t, r, \theta, \phi, p_r, p_\theta)$ with
$p_r = \mathrm{d}r/\mathrm{d}\tau$,
$p_\theta = \mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}\tau$.

The right-hand sides are plain polynomials and rational functions. For
the radial sector, differentiating \eqref{eq:rpotgen} (with
$\Delta' = 2(r - M)$, $P' = 2Er$),
\begin{equation}
R'(r) = 4Er\,P(r) - 2(r-M)\bigl[\mu^2 r^2 + (L_z - aE)^2 + Q\bigr]
- 2\mu^2 r\,\Delta ,
\label{eq:rprime}
\end{equation}
which is exactly what \texttt{radial\_potential\_deriv} returns —
evaluated as written, not by finite differences, so the ``force'' in
\eqref{eq:secondorder} is exact to rounding. Cheap analytic derivatives
are a quiet advantage of the Carter form: the generic Christoffel path
used for Hartle--Thorne (Section~\ref{sec:timelike}) pays roughly an
order of magnitude more per step for the same accuracy, a comparison
quantified in the benchmarks of Chapter~\ref{ch:gpu}.

\subsection{Fundamental frequencies, and why orbits are rosettes}
\label{sec:frequencies}

Separability makes the $r$- and $\theta$-motions individually periodic
in Mino time, with periods
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_r = 2\!\int_{r_{\rm peri}}^{r_{\rm apo}}
\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\sqrt{R(r)}},
\qquad
\Lambda_\theta = 4\!\int_{\theta_{\min}}^{\pi/2}
\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\sqrt{\Theta(\theta)}}
\label{eq:minoperiods}
\end{equation}
\citep{Mino2003}. The azimuth and coordinate time, by \eqref{eq:tphi},
advance by fixed increments per radial (or polar) cycle, so a generic
bound orbit is characterised by three frequencies
$(\omega_r, \omega_\theta, \omega_\phi)$ — and almost never closes:
unless the frequency ratios are rational, the trajectory densely fills
a torus. The observable content of this incommensurability is exactly
the rosette of the primer's Fig.~\ref{fig:dgrosette}: per radial
period, the azimuth advances by $2\pi$ \emph{plus} the apsidal excess
$\Delta\varpi = \omega_\phi/\omega_r \cdot 2\pi - 2\pi$, which for S2
evaluates to the $\PrecSchw'$ per orbit of Part~III. The code never
computes elliptic integrals — the integrator resolves the same
frequencies dynamically — but \eqref{eq:minoperiods} explains a
numerical observation of Chapter~\ref{ch:sstar}: matching a
relativistic orbit to Keplerian initial conditions misassigns the
\emph{radial} period by the frequency mismatch, the 1.6 per cent error
that motivated the Darwin-element initialisation.

The price of differentiating is that \eqref{eq:secondorder} admits
solutions that drift off the true energy shell — precisely the first
integrals
\begin{equation}
p_r^2 = R(r), \qquad p_\theta^2 = \Theta(\theta)
\label{eq:constraints}
\end{equation}
are no longer enforced identically but become \emph{checkable} (and, as
Chapter~\ref{ch:numerics} shows, \emph{re-imposable}) invariants. This
trade — turning-point-free dynamics in exchange for explicit constraint
maintenance — is the central numerical design decision of the project,
and its quantitative payoff is measured in
Figs.~\ref{fig:projboundary}--\ref{fig:drifttol}.

The coordinate singularities of the BL chart at $\theta = 0, \pi$
(where $L_z \neq 0$ makes $\Theta \to -\infty$) are regularised with a
$10^{-8}$ clamp (\texttt{kThetaClamp}); polar-crossing orbits with
$L_z = 0$ pass smoothly.

\section{The time equation, and who owns which clock}
\label{sec:timeclocks}

The $t$-equation of \eqref{eq:tphi} deserves a section of its own,
because three different clocks run through this report and confusing
them is the classic error of the field. \emph{Coordinate time} $t$ is
the bookkeeping label of the BL chart — the far observer's clock, the
time in which the horizon is never crossed and the S2 observation
epochs are stamped. \emph{Proper time} $\tau_{\rm prop}$ ticks on the
moving object itself: for S2 it runs slow by the combined
gravitational-plus-velocity factor $1/u^t$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:disk}
(about seven parts in $10^{4}$ at pericentre — the two
$\sim$$10^{-4}$ redshift terms of Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting} added),
and for photons it does not run at all. \emph{Mino time} $\tau$ is neither: it is a
computational parameter chosen to decouple the equations, with no
clock anywhere reading it. The integrator marches in Mino time,
accumulates coordinate time through \eqref{eq:tphi}, and never needs
proper time for photons at all.

Two physical effects live entirely inside the $t$-equation. First,
its $P(r)/\Delta$ term diverges at the horizon: as a ray approaches
$r_+$, equal steps of Mino time cost ever more coordinate time — the
distant observer sees the approach freeze, which is why the tracer's
$1.001\,r_+$ stop (Chapter~\ref{ch:numerics}) discards only an
infinite tail of frozen bookkeeping, not physics. Second, along
escaping rays the accumulated $t$ minus the flat-space expectation is
finite and positive — strong-field paths arrive \emph{late}, the
Shapiro delay in geodesic dress. The sub-ring echo delay of
Chapter~\ref{ch:ring} and the R{\o}mer solve of
Chapter~\ref{ch:sstar} are both, at bottom, careful audits of this
same accumulated-$t$ ledger, one for photons and one for a star.

\section{Launching rays: from four-momentum to constants}
\label{sec:makenullray}

The ray tracer builds initial conditions from a physical four-momentum
$k^\mu$ at the camera (constructed in the ZAMO tetrad,
Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}). The map to the Carter description
(\texttt{make\_null\_ray}) is purely algebraic: lower the index with the
metric, read off
\begin{equation}
E = -k_t, \qquad L_z = k_\phi,
\end{equation}
and evaluate the Carter constant from the polar momentum,
\begin{equation}
Q = (\Sigma k^\theta)^2
+ \cos^2\theta\left[\frac{L_z^2}{\sin^2\theta} - a^2E^2\right],
\label{eq:qfromk}
\end{equation}
which is \eqref{eq:thetapotgen} solved for $Q$ with
$p_\theta = \Sigma k^\theta$. The Mino momenta are then
$p_r = \Sigma k^r$, $p_\theta = \Sigma k^\theta$ — with \emph{no} sign
ambiguity, since they inherit the sign of the physical launch direction.
(For camera rays $k^\mu$ is the backward-traced tangent of the
\emph{received} photon, so $E < 0$; Section~\ref{sec:camera} and
Appendix~\ref{app:handedness}.)
The identical reconstruction run in reverse
(\texttt{null\_conserved\_errors}) recomputes $(E, L_z, Q)$ from the
evolving state at every step and compares them with the launch values:
the primary health check of the whole physics core (tolerance $10^{-6}$
relative; Chapter~\ref{ch:devhistory}).

\section{Timelike geodesics}
\label{sec:timelike}

For stars, $\mu = 1$ in
\eqref{eq:rpotgen}--\eqref{eq:thetapotgen}: the radial potential gains
the $-\Delta r^2$ mass term and the polar potential's spin term becomes
$a^2(1 - E^2)$. Two independent code paths use this case.

For \emph{Kerr} orbits, the Carter form above applies unchanged and
\texttt{carter\_q} evaluates \eqref{eq:qfromk} (with the $\mu^2$ term)
as a conserved diagnostic. For the \emph{Hartle--Thorne} spacetime of
Chapter~\ref{ch:nohair}, however, no Carter constant exists — the metric
is not separable — so the S-star integrator deliberately uses the
generic second-order geodesic equation
$\mathrm{d}u^\lambda/\mathrm{d}\tau =
-\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu$ with Christoffel symbols from
complex-step differentiation (Chapter~\ref{ch:numerics}), for \emph{any}
metric behind the \texttt{IMetric} interface. The initial $u^t$ is
completed from the spatial velocity by solving the normalisation
quadratic $g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = -1$ for the future-directed root
(\texttt{solve\_ut}), and the same normalisation is the per-step health
check ($|g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu + 1| < 10^{-8}$, Gate 0).

We integrate stellar orbits in the full geodesic equations rather than
any post-Newtonian expansion: at the S2 pericentre ($\sim$1470
Schwarzschild radii, orbital speed 2.6 per cent of $c$) the leading PN
corrections are individually at the $10^{-4}$ level — the same order as
the measured redshift excess itself — so the expansion error budget
would have to be argued term by term, whereas the geodesic integrator's
error budget is simply its conservation diagnostics.

Fig.~\ref{fig:rosettefig} shows the machinery end-to-end: a bound
equatorial orbit with turning points at $8M$ and $25M$, initialised by
the same two-condition Darwin construction the S2 model uses
(Chapter~\ref{ch:sstar}), integrated for six radial periods. The orbit
precesses visibly — this is the same rosette drawn schematically in the
primer — while the four-velocity normalisation drifts at the $10^{-13}$
level, far inside the $10^{-8}$ gate, and the conserved $(E, L)$
reproduce their Darwin target values to eleven decimal places.

\section{Closed-form solutions as an independent check}
\label{sec:closedform}

Because \eqref{eq:firstorder} separates, Kerr geodesics can also be
written in closed form as elliptic integrals
\citep{Darwin1959,RauchBlandford1994,GrallaLupsasca2020}. This project
deliberately does \emph{not} use those solutions in production — the
numerical path generalises to the non-separable Hartle--Thorne metric
where the no-hair test lives, and one code path for all metrics is worth
more than per-metric special cases. The closed forms serve instead as
external anchors: the Schwarzschild critical impact parameter
$\sqrt{27}\,M$, the Bardeen critical curve, and the analytic Lyapunov
exponents of Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow} are all evaluated from them and
compared against traced results — agreement at the $10^{-9}$--$10^{-16}$
level depending on the observable (Chapters~\ref{ch:shadow},
\ref{ch:devhistory}).
