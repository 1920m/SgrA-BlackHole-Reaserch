\chapter{Photon-Ring Substructure}
\label{ch:ring}

Light that grazes the photon shell can complete extra half-orbits before
reaching the camera, stacking exponentially demagnified images of the
disk onto the shadow's rim. This chapter measures that structure
(\texttt{SubRingExtractor::analyze} and \texttt{scan\_widths} in
\texttt{science/photon\_ring/}), connects the measured flux ratios to
the Lyapunov exponents of Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}, extends the
measurements across the spin range, and closes with the axial caustic —
a lensing feature often mistaken for a jet — and the detectability
outlook.

\section{Sub-ring decomposition by crossing count}
\label{sec:subrings}

The tracer counts each ray's equatorial-plane crossings \emph{before}
the crossing that terminates on the opaque disk; that count $n$ is the
ray's sub-ring order \citep{Johnson2020}: $n = 0$ is the direct image,
$n = 1$ the first lensed image (light that passed over or under the
hole once), $n = 2$ the second. Binning image pixels by $n$
(\texttt{analyze}) isolates the orders cleanly
(Fig.~\ref{fig:subrings}): the $n = 1$ ring is a narrow, displaced
band hugging the shadow edge, brightest above and below the shadow at
this inclination, and the $n = 2$ ring is a hair-thin trace on the
critical curve itself. Along the image major axis every pixel is a
direct ($n = 0$) image (Fig.~\ref{fig:profile}): the profile there is
the Doppler-beamed disk, bright on the approaching limb, with the
shadow between the steep inner edges of the direct image.

The measured bolometric ratios in the reference geometry are
$F_0/F_1 = \FluxRatioZeroOne$ and $F_1/F_2 = \FluxRatioOneTwo$. The
steep $n = 1 \to 2$ demagnification is the $e^{-\gamma}$ suppression per
half-orbit derived in Section~\ref{sec:spherical}: with
$\gamma \in [\GammaPolarSgrA, \pi]$ around the ring at this spin, the
naive expectation $F_1/F_2 \sim e^{\bar\gamma} \sim 15$--$23$ applies to
matched image annuli; the much larger \emph{total}-flux ratio also folds
in the shrinking solid angle of the thinner band and the disk's radial
emission profile, which is why total-flux ratios always overshoot the
bare exponential. The $F_0/F_1$ ratio, by contrast, is dominated by how
much of the direct disk image lies far from the critical curve — it
measures the emission model as much as the spacetime.

The orders obey a universal asymptotic law \citep{Johnson2020,
GrallaLupsasca2020}: successive sub-rings converge on the critical
curve geometrically, with the \emph{same} exponent governing all three
observables —
\begin{equation}
w_{n+1} \simeq e^{-\gamma}\,w_n, \qquad
d_n - d_\infty \propto e^{-\gamma n}, \qquad
F_{n+1} \simeq e^{-\gamma}\,F_n \quad (n \gtrsim 1),
\label{eq:universal}
\end{equation}
where $w_n$, $d_n$, $F_n$ are the $n$-th ring's width, diameter, and
flux, $d_\infty$ is the critical-curve diameter, and $\gamma$ is the
Lyapunov exponent of Section~\ref{sec:spherical} for the shell orbit
imaged at that position angle. The law holds only asymptotically — the
$n = 0 \to 1$ step still remembers the emission profile, which is why
$F_0/F_1$ and $F_1/F_2$ differ so strongly in the measurements above —
but from $n = 1$ onward the spacetime dictates everything: width
ratios, diameter convergence, and flux ratios collapse onto
$e^{-\gamma}$. This is what makes the photon ring a \emph{clean} probe
in principle: measure two successive sub-rings and the emission model
cancels out of their ratio, leaving pure Kerr geometry. Our traced
$F_1/F_2$ against the $\gamma$-band of Fig.~\ref{fig:sphericalorbits}
is precisely that consistency check run on synthetic data.

One more exact property deserves note because interferometric
proposals lean on it: the sub-rings are \emph{time-shifted} copies of
the source. A photon in the $n{+}1$ ring lingers one polar half-orbit
longer near the shell than its $n$-ring counterpart; for Schwarzschild
the half-orbit of the $r = 3M$ photon circle takes
$\pi\,r_{\rm ph}^{3/2}/M^{1/2} = 3\sqrt{3}\,\pi\,M \approx 16.3\,M$ of
coordinate time — about $5.8$ minutes at the \sgra{} mass (using the
$21.2$~s per $M$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:f6}). Each sub-ring therefore
replays the disk's flickering with a fixed, mass-determined lag: a
detected ``echo'' at that delay would be a direct, purely geometric
mass measurement, independent of every calibration in
Chapter~\ref{ch:calib}. Our static disk model cannot exhibit the echo,
but the ray-by-ray path-time bookkeeping that would feed such a
simulation is already carried by the tracer's $t$-equation
\eqref{eq:tphi}.

\section{Sub-ring metrics across the spin range}
\label{sec:subringsweep}

The \texttt{--sweep} mode of \texttt{subring\_tool} repeats the full
decomposition at six spins (fixed $i = 80\degr$, $560^2$ rays;
Fig.~\ref{fig:subringsweep}). Two trends carry physical signatures:

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_subring_sweep.pdf}
\caption{Sub-ring metrics versus spin at $i = 80\degr$
(\texttt{subring\_tool --sweep}). Left: bolometric flux ratios; the
$F_1/F_2$ point at $\astar = 0.998$ is omitted (the $n=2$ ring is
unresolved at this image scale). Right: measured $n = 1$ band widths
along three image directions; zero-width entries (band occluded by the
opaque direct image) are omitted.}
\label{fig:subringsweep}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item $F_0/F_1$ rises from $\SubringRatioZeroOneLo$ at $\astar = 0$ to
$\SubringRatioZeroOneHi$ at $\astar = 0.998$: with the ISCO plunging
inward, an ever-larger fraction of the direct emission comes from deep,
strongly beamed radii, outcompeting the lensed band.
\item The $n = 1$ band width below the shadow ($\psi = 270\degr$, the
direction where the band is never occluded) grows monotonically with
spin — the lensed image of the far-side underside widens as the
emitting annulus deepens. Along the other directions the band is
progressively \emph{occluded by the opaque direct image} at high spin:
a real geometric effect of an optically thick disk at high inclination,
recorded honestly as missing measurements rather than zeros.
\end{itemize}

Both behaviours are properties of the exact null geodesics plus a known
emission profile — free of plasma-model degeneracy, at the price of the
thin-disk idealisation discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.

\section{The axial image tower}
\label{sec:axialtower}

Near-edge-on renders at high spin show slim, bright columns of light
along the spin axis, above and below the hole — easily mistaken for the
relativistic jets of radio-galaxy imagery. They are nothing of the
kind, and this section documents what they actually are, because the
distinction is a clean illustration of what a geodesic-plus-thin-disk
model does and does not contain.

The columns are the \emph{polar lensing caustic}. Consider camera rays
launched almost exactly along the spin axis toward the hole: by
axisymmetry, a ray displaced slightly from the axis in \emph{any}
azimuth is bent through the polar region and lands on the far/under
side of the disk. A whole annulus of sky directions therefore maps onto
nearly the same disk material — the Jacobian of the lens map degenerates
on the axis (a caustic of the axisymmetric lens), and magnification
formally diverges there, tamed only by the finite resolution. Stacked
within the column are the $n = 1, 2, \dots$ images of
Section~\ref{sec:subrings} converging toward the polar critical point;
the column is, in effect, the Einstein-ring limit of
Section~\ref{sec:lensing} seen edge-on.

Real astrophysical jets are a different object entirely: magnetically
launched, plasma-loaded outflows powered by mechanisms such as
\citet{BlandfordZnajek1977} energy extraction, requiring
magnetohydrodynamics far outside this model's physics — and \sgra{}
itself shows at most weak evidence for such a jet. The honest statement
is that our columns are pure lensing: they carry no matter, no energy
outflow, and would appear for \emph{any} bright equatorial source around
\emph{any} black hole viewed near the equatorial plane. The
corresponding limitation note appears in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.

\section{Detectability forecast}
\label{sec:ngeht}

The $n = 1$ ring is a target of the next interferometer generation. In
the visibility domain, a thin ring of angular width $w$ contributes a
slowly decaying tail beyond baselines $\sim 1/w$; \citet{Johnson2020}
project that space--ground baselines could isolate the $n = 1$
signature for M87* and \sgra{}. Our measured widths put numbers on the
requirement: at $\astar = 0.94$, $i = 80\degr$ the unoccluded $n = 1$
band spans $\sim 4.5$~mrad of camera angle at $r_{\rm cam} = 10^3\,M$,
i.e.\ $\sim 4.5\,M$ radially — a few $\muas$ at the \sgra{} calibration
of Chapter~\ref{ch:calib}, comfortably inside proposed ngEHT-class
resolution but demanding of dynamic range (the band sits
$\sim\FluxRatioZeroOne\times$ below the direct image in total flux).
Because these maps are exact geodesic quantities on a known spacetime,
they are clean templates for such forecasts; what they deliberately
omit — plasma variability, optical depth, scattering — is the subject
of the limitations chapter.
