\chapter{Kerr Geometry from First Principles}
\label{ch:kerr}

This chapter derives every closed-form Kerr quantity the simulator
implements: the metric and its algebraic structure
(\texttt{kerr\_components} in \texttt{physics/src/metric.cpp}), the
characteristic radii (\texttt{r\_horizon}, \texttt{r\_ergosphere},
\texttt{r\_photon}, \texttt{r\_isco}), and the circular-orbit energetics
(\texttt{omega\_circular}, \texttt{energy\_circular},
\texttt{angmom\_circular}) that feed both the disk model of
Chapter~\ref{ch:disk} and the accretion-efficiency discussion. Everything
here is exact; nothing is expanded in powers of the spin.

\section{The Kerr solution in Boyer--Lindquist coordinates}
\label{sec:kerrmetric}

The unique stationary, axisymmetric, asymptotically flat vacuum black-hole
solution of Einstein's equations is the two-parameter family found by
\citet{Kerr1963}, characterised by mass $M$ and specific angular momentum
$a = J/M$ with $|a| \leq M$. In the coordinates of
\citet{BoyerLindquist1967} $(t, r, \theta, \phi)$, with $G = c = 1$, the
line element is
\begin{align}
\mathrm{d}s^2 ={}& -\left(1-\frac{2Mr}{\Sigma}\right)\mathrm{d}t^2
 - \frac{4Mar\sin^2\theta}{\Sigma}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\phi
 + \frac{\Sigma}{\Delta}\,\mathrm{d}r^2 \nonumber\\
&{}+ \Sigma\,\mathrm{d}\theta^2
 + \frac{A\sin^2\theta}{\Sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\phi^2,
\label{eq:kerr}
\end{align}
built from the three auxiliary functions used throughout this report,
\begin{equation}
\Sigma = r^2+a^2\cos^2\theta, \qquad
\Delta = r^2-2Mr+a^2, \qquad
A = (r^2+a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta\sin^2\theta.
\label{eq:auxfns}
\end{equation}
This is precisely the form implemented by \texttt{kerr\_components}. Two
limits anchor it: $a \to 0$ recovers the Schwarzschild solution in its
standard coordinates, and $M \to 0$ recovers flat spacetime in oblate
spheroidal coordinates — the residual $a$ then only labels a coordinate
choice, which is why all curvature effects below always enter through
products $Ma$ or $M/r$.

\subsection{Metric determinant and inverse}

The $t$--$\phi$ sector of \eqref{eq:kerr} is a $2\times2$ block; its
determinant collapses through the identity
\begin{equation}
g_{tt}\,g_{\phi\phi} - g_{t\phi}^2 = -\Delta \sin^2\theta,
\label{eq:blockdet}
\end{equation}
which the reader can verify by direct substitution of \eqref{eq:auxfns}
(the $A$-terms cancel against the cross term). Multiplying by
$g_{rr}g_{\theta\theta} = \Sigma^2/\Delta$ gives the full determinant
\begin{equation}
\det g = -\Sigma^2 \sin^2\theta .
\label{eq:detg}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:detg} is asserted for random $(r, \theta, a)$ samples
by a dedicated unit test (Chapter~\ref{ch:devhistory}); it is a sensitive
canary because any sign or factor slip in $g_{t\phi}$ breaks it
immediately. The same block structure yields the inverse metric in closed
form — the $t$--$\phi$ block inverts as a $2\times2$ matrix and the
diagonal $r, \theta$ entries invert trivially
(\texttt{invert\_bl\_block} in \texttt{metric.hpp}):
\begin{equation}
g^{tt} = \frac{g_{\phi\phi}}{-\Delta\sin^2\theta}, \quad
g^{t\phi} = \frac{-g_{t\phi}}{-\Delta\sin^2\theta}, \quad
g^{\phi\phi} = \frac{g_{tt}}{-\Delta\sin^2\theta}, \quad
g^{rr} = \frac{\Delta}{\Sigma}, \quad
g^{\theta\theta} = \frac{1}{\Sigma}.
\label{eq:ginv}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Kerr--Schild form}

The Boyer--Lindquist chart is singular at $\Delta = 0$; that surface is a
coordinate artefact, not a curvature singularity. The cleanest way to see
this is the Kerr--Schild form: there exist coordinates
$(\tilde t, x, y, z)$ in which
\begin{equation}
g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + \frac{2Mr^3}{r^4 + a^2 z^2}\, l_\mu l_\nu,
\qquad
l_\mu = \left(1,\ \frac{rx + ay}{r^2+a^2},\ \frac{ry - ax}{r^2+a^2},\
\frac{z}{r}\right),
\label{eq:kerrschild}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the flat metric, $l_\mu$ is null with respect to both
$\eta$ and $g$, and $r(x,y,z)$ is defined implicitly by
$\frac{x^2+y^2}{r^2+a^2} + \frac{z^2}{r^2} = 1$. Every metric component
in \eqref{eq:kerrschild} is manifestly regular at the horizon. The
simulator works in Boyer--Lindquist coordinates throughout — the Carter
separation of Chapter~\ref{ch:geodesics} lives there — and simply stops
rays at $r = 1.001\,r_+$; the Kerr--Schild form is recorded here as the
proof that nothing physical happens at the chart boundary. The true
curvature singularity sits at $\Sigma = 0$, i.e.\ $r = 0$,
$\theta = \pi/2$: a ring of coordinate radius $a$ in the equatorial
plane, safely behind the horizon for all $|a| \le M$.

\section{Killing structure}
\label{sec:killing}

The metric \eqref{eq:kerr} depends on neither $t$ nor $\phi$, so
$\partial_t$ and $\partial_\phi$ are Killing vectors: along any geodesic
with four-momentum $p^\mu$,
\begin{equation}
E \equiv -p_t \quad\text{and}\quad L_z \equiv p_\phi
\label{eq:elz}
\end{equation}
are conserved. Remarkably, Kerr also possesses a \emph{hidden} symmetry
with no coordinate expression: an antisymmetric Killing--Yano tensor
$f_{\mu\nu}$ whose square $K_{\mu\nu} = f_{\mu\lambda}f^{\lambda}{}_{\nu}$
is a rank-2 Killing tensor, $\nabla_{(\lambda}K_{\mu\nu)} = 0$. The
associated quadratic invariant $K_{\mu\nu}p^\mu p^\nu$ furnishes the
fourth constant of motion — Carter's $Q$
\citep{Carter1968} — which is derived from the Hamilton--Jacobi side in
Chapter~\ref{ch:geodesics}. Four constants
$(E, L_z, Q, p_\mu p^\mu)$ for four momentum components is what makes
Kerr geodesics integrable, and hence what makes a machine-precision
ray tracer possible at all.

\section{Frame dragging and the ZAMO}
\label{sec:zamo}

The off-diagonal $g_{t\phi}$ has a direct physical reading. Consider an
observer with zero angular momentum, $u_\phi = 0$ (a ``ZAMO''). Raising
the index,
\begin{equation}
\frac{u^\phi}{u^t} = \frac{g^{\phi t}u_t}{g^{tt}u_t}
= -\frac{g_{t\phi}}{g_{\phi\phi}} \equiv \omega(r, \theta)
= \frac{2Mar}{A},
\label{eq:omega-drag}
\end{equation}
so an observer who \emph{has} no angular momentum nevertheless
\emph{moves}: local inertial frames are dragged around the hole at the
rate $\omega$, falling off as $2Ma/r^3$ far away. The ZAMO is the natural
camera carrier for ray tracing — it exists everywhere outside the
horizon, including inside the ergosphere, and reduces to the static
observer at $a = 0$; its orthonormal tetrad is constructed explicitly in
Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow} (\texttt{zamo\_tetrad}).

\section{Horizons, ergosphere, and the static limit}
\label{sec:radii}

Three characteristic surfaces follow from the metric functions alone.

\emph{Horizons.} The BL chart degenerates where $\Delta = 0$:
\begin{equation}
r_\pm = M \pm \sqrt{M^2 - a^2}.
\label{eq:horizon}
\end{equation}
The outer root $r_+$ is the event horizon (\texttt{r\_horizon}); it
shrinks from $2M$ at $a = 0$ to $M$ at extremality, where the square root
vanishes. For $|a| > M$ there is no horizon at all — a naked
singularity — and the \texttt{KerrMetric} constructor refuses such spins
by design.

\emph{Static limit and ergosphere.} A static observer (fixed $r, \theta,
\phi$) has $u^\mu \propto \partial_t$, which requires $g_{tt} < 0$.
From \eqref{eq:kerr}, $g_{tt} = 0$ where $\Sigma = 2Mr$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
r_E(\theta) = M + \sqrt{M^2 - a^2\cos^2\theta}
\label{eq:ergo}
\end{equation}
(\texttt{r\_ergosphere}). Between $r_+$ and $r_E$ — the ergosphere —
$\partial_t$ is spacelike: no observer can stand still, though escape
remains possible. The surface touches the horizon at the poles and
stands off farthest at the equator ($r_E = 2M$ for every spin), as drawn
in the primer's Fig.~\ref{fig:dganatomy}. Penrose's observation that
orbits of negative energy exist inside this region — allowing rotational
energy extraction — is not exploited by the simulator, but the ZAMO
tetrad's good behaviour there is essential for close-in cameras.

\section{Equatorial photon orbits: the cubic and its trigonometric
solution}
\label{sec:photonorbit}

In the equatorial plane ($\theta = \pi/2$, $Q = 0$) the radial potential
of Chapter~\ref{ch:geodesics} for a photon of impact parameter
$b = L_z/E$ reduces to
\begin{equation}
\frac{R(r)}{E^2} = \left(r^2 + a^2 - a b\right)^2
 - \Delta\,(b - a)^2 .
\label{eq:Req}
\end{equation}
A circular photon orbit requires a double root: $R(r) = R'(r) = 0$.
Dividing the two conditions eliminates $b$; after simplification
(worked in full in Appendix~\ref{app:algebra}) the radius of the
circular orbit satisfies
\begin{equation}
r^{3/2} - 3M r^{1/2} \pm 2a\sqrt{M} = 0,
\label{eq:photoncubic}
\end{equation}
a cubic in $\sqrt{r}$ (upper sign prograde). Depressed cubics of the
form $x^3 - 3px + q = 0$ with three real roots are solved by the
trigonometric substitution $x = 2\sqrt{p}\cos\vartheta$, which turns the
equation into $\cos 3\vartheta = -q/(2p^{3/2})$. Applying it with
$x = \sqrt{r/M}$, $p = 1$, $q = \pm 2a/M$ gives the closed form
implemented in \texttt{r\_photon}:
\begin{equation}
r_{\rm ph}^{\pm} = 2M\left\{1 +
\cos\!\left[\tfrac{2}{3}\cos^{-1}\!\left(\mp\frac{a}{M}\right)\right]
\right\}.
\label{eq:rph}
\end{equation}
At $a = 0$ both signs give $\cos^{-1}(0) = \pi/2$ and
$r_{\rm ph} = 2M(1 + \cos 60^\circ) = 3M$, the Schwarzschild photon
sphere. At extremality the prograde orbit reaches $r_{\rm ph}^+ = M$
(coincident in coordinate radius with the horizon) while the retrograde
orbit retreats to $4M$: frame dragging assists co-rotating light and
punishes counter-rotating light, the asymmetry visible in the traced
trajectories of Fig.~\ref{fig:trajschw} and, ultimately, in the
displaced shadow of Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}. The corresponding critical
impact parameters follow from back-substituting \eqref{eq:rph} into the
double-root conditions; they reappear as the Bardeen critical curve in
Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}.

\section{Circular timelike orbits: $\Omega$, $E$, $L$}
\label{sec:energetics}

For massive particles on circular equatorial orbits, the geodesic
equation reduces to one algebraic condition. Writing the orbital angular
velocity $\Omega = u^\phi/u^t$ and demanding
$\mathrm{d}^2r/\mathrm{d}\tau^2 = 0$ yields (equivalently: extremising
the effective potential of Fig.~\ref{fig:timelikepot})
\begin{equation}
\Omega_\pm = \frac{\pm\sqrt{M}}{r^{3/2} \pm a\sqrt{M}},
\label{eq:omegacirc}
\end{equation}
the relativistic generalisation of Kepler's third law
(\texttt{omega\_circular}); the $a$-term in the denominator is pure
frame dragging. The four-velocity normalisation then fixes $u^t$, and
lowering indices gives the specific energy and angular momentum
\citep{BardeenPressTeukolsky1972}:
\begin{align}
E(r) &= \frac{1 - 2M/r \pm a\sqrt{M/r^3}}
            {\sqrt{1 - 3M/r \pm 2a\sqrt{M/r^3}}},
\label{eq:ecirc}\\[2pt]
L(r) &= \frac{\pm\sqrt{Mr}\,\bigl(1 + a^2/r^2 \mp 2a\sqrt{M/r^3}\bigr)}
            {\sqrt{1 - 3M/r \pm 2a\sqrt{M/r^3}}}
\label{eq:lcirc}
\end{align}
(\texttt{energy\_circular}, \texttt{angmom\_circular}; upper signs
prograde). Fig.~\ref{fig:energetics} plots all three across the spin
range. Two structural features matter downstream. First, the common
denominator vanishes at the \emph{photon orbit} — circular orbits become
null there, and between the photon orbit and the ISCO they exist but are
unstable. Second, $E(r)$ has a minimum exactly at the ISCO, which is the
cleanest way to locate it.

\section{The ISCO and the marginally bound orbit}
\label{sec:isco}

Stability of a circular orbit requires the effective potential's
extremum to be a minimum; the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) is
the inflection point where $\partial^2 V/\partial r^2 = 0$ joins the
circular-orbit conditions. Carrying out the algebra (the full reduction
is in Appendix~\ref{app:algebra}) collapses the triple condition to a
quartic in $\sqrt{r}$ whose relevant root is expressed through the
celebrated auxiliary quantities of \citet{BardeenPressTeukolsky1972}:
\begin{align}
Z_1 &= 1 + (1-\astar^2)^{1/3}\left[(1+\astar)^{1/3} +
      (1-\astar)^{1/3}\right],\nonumber\\
Z_2 &= \sqrt{3\astar^2 + Z_1^2},\nonumber\\
r_{\rm ISCO} &= M\left[3 + Z_2 \mp
      \sqrt{(3-Z_1)(3+Z_1+2Z_2)}\right],
\label{eq:isco}
\end{align}
with the upper sign prograde (\texttt{r\_isco}). The checks the test
suite pins (Chapter~\ref{ch:devhistory}): at $\astar = 0$,
$Z_1 = 3$, $Z_2 = 3$ and $r_{\rm ISCO} = 6M$ exactly; as
$\astar \to 1$ the prograde ISCO slides to $M$ while the retrograde one
recedes to $9M$; and the prograde radius decreases monotonically with
spin (Fig.~\ref{fig:radiispin}).

A second marginal radius appears in accretion theory: the
\emph{marginally bound} orbit, where $E = 1$ — a particle dropped from
rest at infinity can just circularise. Setting $E = 1$ in
\eqref{eq:ecirc} gives
\begin{equation}
r_{\rm mb}^{\pm} = 2M \mp a + 2\sqrt{M(M \mp a)},
\label{eq:rmb}
\end{equation}
lying between the photon orbit and the ISCO for every spin
(Fig.~\ref{fig:radiispin}).

\section{Binding energy and accretion efficiency}
\label{sec:efficiency}

Equation~\eqref{eq:ecirc} carries a direct astrophysical payload. Gas
spiralling quasi-circularly through a disk arrives at the ISCO with
specific energy $E_{\rm ISCO}$, having radiated the binding energy
\begin{equation}
\eta = 1 - E_{\rm ISCO}
\label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
per unit rest mass. For Schwarzschild,
$E_{\rm ISCO} = \sqrt{8/9}$ and $\eta = 1 - 2\sqrt{2}/3 =
\EtaSchw\,\%$. At the \sgra{} working spin $\astar = 0.94$ the
efficiency is $\EtaSgrA\,\%$, and at $\astar = 0.998$ it reaches
$\EtaNearExtremal\,\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:efficiency}; values generated by
\texttt{physics\_tables} from \eqref{eq:ecirc} and \eqref{eq:isco}) —
an order of magnitude beyond nuclear fusion's $0.7\%$, which is why
accreting black holes are the most efficient steady engines known. The
same $E$ and $L$ profiles drive the Page--Thorne dissipation integral of
Chapter~\ref{ch:disk}: the disk's radial brightness profile is nothing
but the gradient of this binding-energy release, corrected for the
torque bookkeeping.
