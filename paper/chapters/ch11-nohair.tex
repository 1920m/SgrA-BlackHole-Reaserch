\chapter{A No-Hair Test with a Free Quadrupole}
\label{ch:nohair}

The no-hair theorem is only worth stating if it can fail. This chapter
builds the failure mode into the spacetime — one extra parameter, the
quadrupole surplus $\epsq$ — and lets the S2 data set its value
(\texttt{physics/src/hartle\_thorne.cpp};
\texttt{OrbitFitter::fit\_nohair}).

\section{Multipole moments and the no-hair statement}
\label{sec:multipoles}

Any stationary, asymptotically flat spacetime is characterised far from
the source by two towers of multipole moments (Geroch--Hansen): the mass
moments $M_0 = M, M_2, M_4, \dots$ and the current moments
$S_1 = J, S_3, \dots$ (odd mass moments and even current moments vanish
for reflection-symmetric sources). For a fluid star the towers are
essentially free — they encode the internal mass distribution. The
no-hair theorem states that for a Kerr black hole the \emph{entire}
double tower collapses to two numbers:
\begin{equation}
M_\ell + i S_\ell = M\,(ia)^\ell
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
M_2 = -M a^2,\;\; S_3 = -M a^3,\;\dots
\label{eq:nohairmoments}
\end{equation}
The first non-trivial prediction is the mass quadrupole
$M_2 = -Ma^2$: measure $M$, $a$ and $M_2$ \emph{independently} and
general relativity's black-hole hypothesis is falsifiable. Following
\citet{Will2008} we parametrise the violation as
\begin{equation}
Q_{\rm tot} = -M a^2 + \epsq M^3,
\label{eq:qtot}
\end{equation}
so $\epsq = 0$ is Kerr and $|\epsq|$ measures the surplus squashing of
the field in natural units.

\section{The Hartle--Thorne / quasi-Kerr metric}
\label{sec:ht}

A spacetime realising \eqref{eq:qtot} is furnished by the slow-rotation
solution of \citet{HartleThorne1968}, valid through
$\mathcal{O}(a^2)$ with the quadrupole free — adequate here because the
quadrupole's orbital effect is itself a small perturbation at S2 radii.
We implement the quasi-Kerr form of \citet{GlampedakisBabak2006}: the
deviation enters as a perturbation of the \emph{inverse} metric,
\begin{equation}
g^{ab} = g^{ab}_{\rm Kerr} + \epsq\, h^{ab},
\label{eq:quasikerr}
\end{equation}
where the non-zero $h^{ab}$ components are built from two radial
structure functions $F_1(r), F_2(r)$ multiplying
$(1 - 3\cos^2\theta)$ angular factors — the quadrupole's $P_2$
signature. Perturbing the inverse metric is a deliberate choice: the
geodesic Hamiltonian is $\tfrac12 g^{ab}p_a p_b$, so \eqref{eq:quasikerr}
feeds the equations of motion linearly and the $\epsq \to 0$ limit is
exact by construction. A dedicated unit test asserts that limit at the
$10^{-12}$ level component-by-component (Gate 0;
Chapter~\ref{ch:devhistory}).

The structure functions carry a numerical trap that is worth recording.
$F_1$ and $F_2$ are combinations of low-order polynomials and
logarithms $\ln\!\bigl(r/(r-2M)\bigr)$ arranged so that the
\emph{leading five} powers of $M/r$ cancel — the quadrupole correction
must fall off as $r^{-3}$ relative to the monopole, and the cancellation
enforcing this is catastrophic in floating point: at $r = 50\,M$ the
individual terms are $\sim 10^{5}$ times the surviving result, at
$r = 300\,M$ (an S2 apocentre distance scale) $\sim 10^{10}$ times.
The implementation therefore switches, beyond $r = 9M$, to the
asymptotic series of the cancelled result, with the switch radius chosen
where the two evaluations agree to $10^{-10}$; a branch-continuity unit
test pins the joint (Chapter~\ref{ch:devhistory}). Because the metric is
not Carter-separable, all orbits in this spacetime run through the
generic Christoffel integrator with complex-step derivatives
(Chapters~\ref{ch:geodesics}--\ref{ch:numerics}) — the reason that
integrator exists.

\section{The quadrupole precession anchor}
\label{sec:quadprec}

One closed-form consequence anchors the sign and scale of everything: a
quadrupole $\tilde q = Q/M^3$ adds, for an equatorial orbit of
semilatus rectum $p$, a pericentre advance
\begin{equation}
\delta\omega_Q = -3\pi\,\tilde q\,\Bigl(\frac{M}{p}\Bigr)^{2}
\quad\text{per orbit}
\label{eq:quadprecession}
\end{equation}
\citep{Will2008} — retrograde for an oblate source ($\tilde q < 0$
advances, $\epsq > 0$ retards; the Newtonian $-QP_2/r^3$ potential
perturbation gives the same result, which is the fastest way to fix the
sign). The implementation's sign convention is pinned by a
\emph{two-sided} test: the analytic \eqref{eq:quadprecession}
(\texttt{GRPrecession::quadrupole}) against the numerically measured
apsidal advance of an integrated quasi-Kerr orbit, for both signs of
$\epsq$ — a pairing that once caught a real sign error in $h^{ab}$
(the bug-hunt narrative appears in Chapter~\ref{ch:devhistory}).
Fig.~\ref{fig:precdecomp} places \eqref{eq:quadprecession} on the
precession ladder: at S2's $p \approx 5400\,M$, one unit of $\epsq$
moves the pericentre by $0.07''$ per orbit, five orders below the
Schwarzschild term.

\section{Profile-likelihood constraint from S2}
\label{sec:nohairresult}

Re-fitting the S2 data in the quasi-Kerr spacetime with $\epsq$ as a
fourteenth parameter uses a profile likelihood: $\epsq$ is stepped over
a grid, the remaining thirteen parameters are re-optimised at each step
(\texttt{OrbitFitter::fit\_nohair}), and
$\Delta\chi^2(\epsq) = \chi^2(\epsq) - \chi^2_{\rm min}$ is read
against the $\Delta\chi^2 = 1$ (68 per cent) and $3.84$ (95 per cent)
levels. The profile (Fig.~\ref{fig:nohair}) is a clean parabola centred
near zero: the best fit is $\epsq = \EpsQBest\times10^{5}$ with
$\sigma(\epsq) = \EpsQSigma\times10^{5}$ and a 95 per cent half-width
of $\EpsQCiNinetyFive\times10^{5}$. The data are fully consistent with
the no-hair value, and the marginal $\chi^2_\nu$ is unchanged from the
Kerr fit — the extra degree of freedom buys nothing, exactly as
expected if Kerr is the true spacetime.

The MCMC of Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}, re-run with the fourteenth
parameter, gives the same answer from a global sampling:
$\epsq = \McmcEpsQMed^{+\McmcEpsQHi}_{-\McmcEpsQLo}\times10^{5}$
(16/50/84 percentiles), and the joint $(\epsq, M)$ posterior
(Fig.~\ref{fig:nohaircontours}) shows the Kerr value comfortably inside
the 68 per cent contour with no significant $\epsq$--$M$ leakage — the
quadrupole is not silently absorbing mass mis-estimates.

\section{Why the constraint is weak, and how it improves}
\label{sec:nohairforecast}

The constraint is weak in absolute terms because of the
$(M/p)^2$ suppression in \eqref{eq:quadprecession}. Working the
numbers: $M/p = 1.86\times10^{-4}$ for S2, so one unit of $\epsq$ moves
the pericentre by $3.3\times10^{-7}$~rad per orbit — $0.07''$ —
against an astrometric sensitivity to $\omega$ of order $0.5\degr$ per
orbit (Table~\ref{tab:fit}). Inverting, the expected reach is
$\sigma(\epsq) \sim 10^{5}$, exactly the order the profile likelihood
recovers: a single star at $\sim$3000 gravitational radii is simply far
from the quadrupole's domain of influence. The value of the exercise is
methodological — the machinery applies unchanged to closer stars.
Continued monitoring helps: propagating the fitted covariance forward,
ten further S2 orbits of the existing quality shrink the uncertainty to
$\sigma(\epsq) \approx \EpsQForecastTenOrbits\times10^{5}$, a factor
$\sim$3. A qualitative jump requires a confirmed star well inside S2's
orbit: the $(M/p)^2$ scaling means a star at a third of S2's pericentre
is an order of magnitude more sensitive per orbit, with a shorter
period compounding the gain. The reported candidate S62, with a claimed
9.9-yr period and $\sim$36~au pericentre, would be such a star, but we
emphasise: \emph{S62 is a contested orbit — its identification is
unconfirmed and may be a misidentification of S29 or a blend artefact};
we therefore build no quantitative forecast on it, and any future use
of S62 in this context must carry the same caveat.
