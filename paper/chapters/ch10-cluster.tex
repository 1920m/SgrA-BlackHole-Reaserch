\chapter{The Wider S-Star Cluster: A Joint Mass Fit}
\label{ch:cluster}

Every star orbiting \sgra{} orbits the \emph{same} black hole at the
\emph{same} distance. A fit that shares one $(M, R_0)$ and one
reference frame across many stars therefore multiplies the constraint —
and, as this chapter shows concretely, rescues stars whose individual
arcs are too short to weigh the hole at all. This is the multi-star
programme of \citet{Gillessen2017} and \citet{Gravity2022} reproduced
inside our own machinery (\texttt{fit\_cluster} in
\texttt{science/orbit\_fitter/}, over the data fetched star-by-star by
the refactored \texttt{fetch\_gillessen\_data.py}).

\section{Data and model}
\label{sec:clusterdata}

The VizieR machine-readable Table~5 of \citet{Gillessen2017} carries
per-epoch astrometry and radial velocities for seventeen S-stars in
per-star columns; the fetch script extracts any requested star into the
same CSV format the S2 pipeline uses. The joint fit uses five:
\textbf{S2} (145 astrometric epochs + 44 RVs, $P = 16$~yr — the anchor,
observed through more than a full orbit), \textbf{S1} (161 + 29,
$P \approx 166$~yr), \textbf{S8} (166 + 29, $P \approx 93$~yr),
\textbf{S12} (158 + 27, $P \approx 59$~yr) and \textbf{S13} (127 + 22,
$P \approx 49$~yr) — all with published uncertainties used as-is. The
four companions contribute \emph{partial arcs}: between 1992 and 2016
they cover from a sixth to half of an orbit.

The shared-potential model has $7 + 6N$ parameters for $N$ stars: one
global block — $M$, $R_0$, and the five reference-frame parameters
$(x_0, y_0, v_x, v_y, v_z)$ with the \citet{Plewa2015} priors applied
once — plus six osculating elements per star. Each star's residuals go
through the identical full-GR forward model of Chapter~\ref{ch:sstar}
(Darwin elements, geodesic integration, R{\o}mer delay, relativistic
redshift), assembled into one Ceres problem with per-star parameter
blocks so the solver exploits the sparsity (star $k$'s elements touch
only star $k$'s residuals). Starting elements are the Table~3 values of
\citet{Gillessen2017}, refined per star before the joint solve; a
starting-$\chi^2$ probe over the four $(\Omega, \omega) \pm 180\degr$
orientation conventions guards against convention mismatches.

\section{Why partial arcs need a shared potential}
\label{sec:partialarc}

The single-star column of Fig.~\ref{fig:clusterfig} is the
demonstration. Fitted alone with the full 13-parameter model, S2
recovers $M = \FitMass \pm \ClusterMassErrSTwoOnly \times10^6\,\msun$ —
the Phase-4 result. S1, with a $\sim$15 per cent arc of a 166-year
orbit, still manages a mass because its arc includes strong curvature.
But S8's single fit \emph{fails to converge} (the optimiser runs into
the parameter bounds), and S12's ``converges'' to a mass three sigma
below everyone else's with an uncertainty half the value itself: with a
short arc, mass, distance, period and eccentricity trade against each
other almost freely — the fit is happy, the parameters are meaningless.
This is not a defect of the optimiser; it is genuine degeneracy of the
truncated data, and it is the concrete, reproducible illustration of why
single-short-arc ``measurements'' of \sgra's mass should never be taken
at face value.

The joint fit changes the game: $M$ and $R_0$ are pinned by S2's
complete orbit, and each partial-arc star then only needs its six
elements — for which even a short arc with RVs suffices. In exchange,
every additional star's data sharpens the \emph{global} block.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:clusterresults}

The five-star joint fit converges with
$\chi^2_\nu = \ClusterChiRed$ over all
$2\times757$ astrometric + 151 RV residuals and gives
\begin{equation}
M = \ClusterMass \pm \ClusterMassErr \times 10^{6}\,\msun,
\qquad
R_0 = \ClusterDist \pm \ClusterDistErr~\mathrm{pc}.
\end{equation}
Against the S2-only fit the mass uncertainty shrinks by
\ClusterMassImprovePct{} per cent and the distance uncertainty by
\ClusterDistImprovePct{} per cent — with \emph{no new instrument},
purely by demanding that five stars share one potential. The joint mass
sits $\ClusterMassNSigma\sigma$ from the GRAVITY multi-orbit
determination of $4.297\pm0.012\times10^6\,\msun$ \citep{Gravity2022};
given that our input is the 1992--2016 imaging-era compilation only —
none of the GRAVITY beam astrometry — the agreement is a genuine
cross-check, not a tautology. The middle rungs of the convergence
ladder (Fig.~\ref{fig:clusterfig}, right; \texttt{cluster\_fit.json}
\texttt{subsets}) show the expected pattern: the biggest single gain
comes from adding the first independent star (S1), with diminishing —
but monotonic — returns thereafter.

Two honest caveats bound the result. First, our error model is the
published per-epoch uncertainties with the \citet{Plewa2015} frame
priors — no error rescaling and no systematic-floor terms, whereas the
professional multi-star fits marginalise over per-instrument offsets;
our smaller formal uncertainty should be read in that light. Second,
the companion stars are treated, like S2, as test particles in a clean
Kerr-limit potential; extended mass inside their apocentres, which the
professional fits constrain simultaneously, is not modelled — its
absence is part of the residual budget, not the quoted errors.

We note for completeness that the contested star S62 — claimed at a
9.9-yr period well inside S2's orbit, but plausibly a misidentification
of S29 or a blend artefact — is \emph{not} included and would be the
single most valuable addition if its orbit were ever confirmed
(Chapter~\ref{ch:nohair} quantifies why); any future use of S62 in this
pipeline must carry the same contested-orbit caveat.
