\chapter{Spinning Black Holes and the Shadow}
\label{ch:f3}

\section{Black holes spin}

Almost everything in the universe rotates — planets, stars, whole
galaxies — and when a rotating object collapses, its spin is not lost. It
concentrates, the way a figure skater speeds up when she pulls her arms
in. Black holes born from collapsing, swirling matter are therefore
expected to spin, often ferociously.

A non-spinning black hole is described exactly by Schwarzschild's 1915
solution of Chapter~\ref{ch:f1}. The spinning case resisted for another
half-century, until Roy Kerr found the exact solution in 1963. The
\emph{Kerr metric} — the rulebook of Chapter~\ref{ch:f2} for a spinning
hole — is the mathematical foundation of this entire report, and it says
that a black hole is completely described by just two numbers: its mass
and its spin. The spin is usually quoted as a dimensionless number
$\astar$ between 0 (not spinning) and 1 (the theoretical maximum, spinning
so fast the horizon would tear open). For \sgra{} the working estimate
used throughout this report is $\astar = 0.94$ — a rapid spinner.

\section{Frame dragging: spacetime as a whirlpool}

A spinning mass does something Newton never imagined: it drags spacetime
around with it. Close to a spinning black hole, ``standing still'' is
simply not on the menu — space itself circulates like water around a
drain, and everything in it, light included, is carried along. This
\emph{frame dragging} has been measured in Earth orbit (by the Gravity
Probe~B gyroscopes and the LAGEOS satellites) at the level of fractions of
a degree per century; near \sgra{} it becomes a dominant feature of the
landscape (Fig.~\ref{fig:dgframedrag}).

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/dg_frame_drag.pdf}
\caption{Frame dragging around a black hole with $\astar=0.94$, computed
from the Kerr rulebook. Each arrow shows how far a ``stationary'' marker
at that radius is swept around per unit time; inside the orange boundary
(the ergosphere) refusing to rotate is impossible.}
\label{fig:dgframedrag}
\end{figure}

There is a region, outside the horizon but close to it, where the dragging
is so strong that \emph{nothing} can hold still — to hover without
circulating you would need to move faster than light. This region is the
\emph{ergosphere}, a flattened shell thickest over the equator
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dganatomy}). Objects inside it can still escape — it is
not a point of no return — but they cannot avoid being swept around.

\section{The anatomy of a spinning hole}

Fig.~\ref{fig:dganatomy} labels the landmarks that Part~II computes
exactly, for a non-spinning and a rapidly spinning hole side by side.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/dg_kerr_anatomy.pdf}
\caption{Cross-sections through a non-spinning ($\astar=0$) and a rapidly
spinning ($\astar=0.94$) black hole, drawn to scale from the exact
formulas of Chapter~\ref{ch:kerr}. Spin shrinks the horizon, opens up an
ergosphere, splits the photon orbit into prograde/retrograde pairs, and
pulls the innermost stable orbit (ISCO) dramatically inward.}
\label{fig:dganatomy}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{event horizon} (black): the point of no return. Spin
shrinks it, from $2\,M$ at $\astar=0$ toward $1\,M$ at the extreme.
\item The \emph{ergosphere} (orange): the no-standing-still zone,
touching the horizon at the poles and bulging at the equator.
\item The \emph{photon orbits} (blue): radii where light can circle the
hole. For a spinning hole there are two equatorial ones — a closer orbit
for light moving \emph{with} the rotation (\emph{prograde}) and a farther
one for light moving \emph{against} it (\emph{retrograde}). Spacetime's
whirlpool helps the co-rotating light hold a tighter circle.
\item The \emph{ISCO} (green), the innermost stable circular orbit: the
closest circle on which matter can orbit steadily. Any closer and the
slightest nudge sends it spiralling in. The ISCO is the inner edge of the
glowing disk of Chapter~\ref{ch:f4}, and it moves inward spectacularly
with spin — from $6\,M$ for $\astar=0$ to about $2\,M$ at
$\astar=0.94$. Much of what changes visually with spin in our renders
traces back to this single fact.
\end{itemize}

\section{The shadow and the photon ring}

Now surround the hole with light — a glowing disk, or simply the stars
behind it — and ask what a distant camera sees. Chapter~\ref{ch:f2}
showed that rays aimed too close are captured. Point the camera at the
hole, and there is a patch of sky, for every pixel of which the answer is
``that line of sight ends on the horizon''. That dark patch is the
\emph{shadow} (Fig.~\ref{fig:dgshadow}).

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/dg_shadow_formation.pdf}
\caption{How the shadow forms, computed from the exact bending equation.
Green rays escape (bent); the orange critical ray circles on the photon
sphere; red rays are captured. The shadow is the set of camera directions
whose rays end on the horizon — its edge is light that grazed the photon
sphere.}
\label{fig:dgshadow}
\end{figure}

Three facts about the shadow do a great deal of work in this report.

First, \emph{the shadow is bigger than the horizon}. Light does not have
to hit the horizon to be lost — anything aimed within the critical
distance spirals in. For a non-spinning hole the shadow's radius is
$\sqrt{27}\approx 5.2\,M$, about 2.6 times the horizon radius. Our
simulator reproduces this number to sixteen digits
(Chapter~\ref{ch:shadow}), which is the sort of check that separates a
scientific instrument from a pretty picture.

Second, \emph{the shadow's edge is made of light that almost stayed}.
Rays that graze the critical boundary can circle the hole once, twice,
three times before escaping to the camera. Each extra half-turn stacks
another, exponentially thinner and fainter image of the whole sky (and of
the disk) right at the shadow's rim. This onion of nested images is the
\emph{photon ring}, and its sub-rings are analysed quantitatively in
Chapter~\ref{ch:ring}. The Event Horizon Telescope's measurement of this
ring for \sgra{} — $51.8\pm2.3\,\muas$ across — is one of the two
observational anchors of this whole project.

Third, \emph{spin distorts the shadow}. Frame dragging lets prograde
light survive closer in, so the shadow's edge on the side rotating toward
you pulls inward: the silhouette flattens on one side and shifts
sideways, by an amount our simulator measures precisely
(Chapters~\ref{ch:shadow}, \ref{ch:calib}). Viewed nearly pole-on — our
actual view of \sgra{} — the distortion is frustratingly small, a few
per cent, which is why the ring's \emph{size} alone cannot pin down the
spin (the ``spin--inclination degeneracy'' that
Chapter~\ref{ch:calib} maps out).

\begin{termsbox}
\item[Kerr metric] The exact spacetime of a spinning black hole (Kerr
1963); fully specified by mass and spin.
\item[Spin parameter $\astar$] Dimensionless spin from 0 to 1; \sgra{}
is modelled at $\astar = 0.94$.
\item[Frame dragging] The whirlpool-like circulation of spacetime around
a spinning mass.
\item[Ergosphere] The region where standing still is impossible; escape
is still possible from inside it.
\item[Prograde / retrograde] Moving with / against the black hole's
rotation; prograde orbits survive closer in.
\item[Photon sphere / photon orbits] Radii at which light circles the
hole; unstable, so grazing light eventually escapes or falls.
\item[ISCO] Innermost stable circular orbit — the inner edge of the
disk; moves from $6\,M$ (no spin) toward the horizon as spin grows.
\item[Shadow] The dark patch of captured lines of sight; radius
$\sqrt{27}\,M$ for no spin, subtly reshaped by spin.
\item[Photon ring] The stack of exponentially fainter images of the
scene hugging the shadow's edge.
\end{termsbox}
