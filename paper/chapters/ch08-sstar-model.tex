\chapter{Timelike Orbits and the S-Star Forward Model}
\label{ch:sstar}

The S2 fit of Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting} stands on a forward model: given
thirteen parameters, predict every measured position and velocity. This
chapter derives that model layer by layer
(\texttt{SkyProjection::S2Model} in \texttt{science/orbit\_fitter/}):
initial conditions from exact turning points, integration and sampling,
sky projection, the R{\o}mer delay, and the relativistic redshift
factorisation (\texttt{redshift\_terms},
\texttt{SpectralLineFitter::predict}).

\section{The forward model at a glance}
\label{sec:forwardmodel}

One model evaluation proceeds: (i) convert the osculating elements
$(a, e, i, \Omega, \omega, t_{\rm peri})$ plus $(M, R_0)$ into exact
initial conditions (Section~\ref{sec:darwin}); (ii) integrate the
timelike geodesic through the observation window with the generic
Christoffel integrator of Chapter~\ref{ch:geodesics} (this path works
for Kerr \emph{and} Hartle--Thorne, which the no-hair fit requires);
(iii) interpolate the cached trajectory at each requested emission time
(cubic Hermite on position and velocity, \texttt{sample\_at});
(iv) project onto the sky and apply the reference-frame offsets
$(x_0, y_0, v_x, v_y)$; (v) solve the R{\o}mer delay per epoch
(Section~\ref{sec:romer}); (vi) evaluate the observed radial velocity
including all relativistic terms (Section~\ref{sec:spectro}). Steps
(i)--(iii) run once per parameter vector; steps (iv)--(vi) per data
epoch. One full evaluation against the S2 dataset costs 3.7~ms — the
number that made the MCMC of Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting} practical.

\section{Orbital elements from radial turning points}
\label{sec:darwin}

The osculating elements define the pericentre and apocentre radii
$r_p = \tilde a(1-e)$, $r_a = \tilde a(1+e)$ (with $\tilde a$ the
physical semi-major axis from $a_{\rm mas}$ and $R_0$). The exact
relativistic orbit with these turning points has $E$ and $L_z$ fixed by
the two conditions
\begin{equation}
R(r_p) = 0, \qquad R(r_a) = 0,
\label{eq:turningconds}
\end{equation}
with $R$ the timelike radial potential \eqref{eq:rpotgen} — the
construction of \citet{Darwin1959}. For the equatorial-plane
integration frame the code uses ($Q = 0$; the orbit is subsequently
rotated to the sky by $(i, \Omega, \omega)$), at $a = 0$ the potential
is $R = E^2r^4 - \Delta(r^2 + L_z^2)$ and \eqref{eq:turningconds} is a
$2\times2$ \emph{linear} system in $(E^2, L_z^2)$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix} r_p^4 & -\Delta_p \\ r_a^4 & -\Delta_a \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} E^2 \\ L_z^2 \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} \Delta_p r_p^2 \\ \Delta_a r_a^2 \end{pmatrix}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
E^2 = \frac{\Delta_p\Delta_a\,(r_a^2 - r_p^2)}
           {\Delta_p r_a^4 - \Delta_a r_p^4},
\qquad
L_z^2 = \frac{r_p^4\,\Delta_a r_a^2 - \Delta_p r_p^2\, r_a^4}
             {\Delta_p r_a^4 - \Delta_a r_p^4}
\label{eq:darwinsystem}
\end{equation}
(Cramer's rule, $\Delta_{p,a} = \Delta(r_{p,a})$). The implementation
writes the same system in the equivalent form
$E^2 = (1 - 2M/r)(1 + L^2/r^2)$ at both radii and solves for $L^2$
first — algebraically identical, but numerically better conditioned at
S2-like radii ($r \sim 10^3$--$10^4\,M$) because it avoids
differencing the large $r^4$ terms. The rosette integration of
Fig.~\ref{fig:rosettefig}
validated exactly this construction: the integrated orbit's conserved
$(E, L)$ match \eqref{eq:darwinsystem} to eleven decimal places.

\emph{Why not Keplerian initial conditions?} The obvious shortcut —
start at pericentre with the Newtonian vis-viva speed — mis-states the
\emph{relativistic} orbit: with the same $(r_p, v_p)$ the GR orbit has a
different apocentre and, at S2's eccentricity, an orbital period wrong
by 1.6 per cent. The measured S2 period is known to $0.014$ per cent,
so the shortcut is two orders of magnitude too crude — a bug found by
exactly this discrepancy during Phase 4 and pinned by a regression test
since (the narrative is told in Chapter~\ref{ch:devhistory}). The
turning-point construction, by contrast, reproduces the requested
geometry exactly by definition.

\section{Sky projection and the R{\o}mer delay}
\label{sec:romer}

The integrated orbit lives in BH-centred coordinates; the observables
live on the sky. The rotation by $(i, \Omega, \omega)$ maps the orbital
plane to East--North offsets and a line-of-sight coordinate $z$
(positive away from us), scaled by $R_0$ into angles; the fitted frame
parameters $(x_0, y_0, v_x, v_y)$ absorb the residual tie between the
infrared frame and the radio position of \sgra{}, and $v_z$ the
line-of-sight zero point.

Light from different orbit phases travels different distances: an epoch
observed at $t_{\rm obs}$ was \emph{emitted} at $t_{\rm em}$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
t_{\rm em} = t_{\rm obs} - \frac{z(t_{\rm em})}{c},
\label{eq:romer}
\end{equation}
the classical R{\o}mer delay (first used by R{\o}mer in 1676 to measure
$c$ from Jupiter's moons; here it is $c$ that is known and the geometry
that matters). Equation \eqref{eq:romer} is an implicit equation for
$t_{\rm em}$; the code solves it by fixed-point iteration
$t^{(k+1)} = t_{\rm obs} - z(t^{(k)})/c$, which contracts with rate
$|\dot z/c| = |\beta_z| \lesssim 0.026$ for S2 — guaranteeing
convergence in 2--3 iterations to sub-second accuracy. For S2 the delay
spans about $\pm 4$ light-days across the orbit
(Fig.~\ref{fig:romer}); ignoring it would shift pericentre-passage
timing by days against astrometry measured to weeks — a measurable
model error, which is why the delay is applied to every epoch of every
star.

\section{The spectroscopic observable}
\label{sec:spectro}

The measured quantity for S2 spectroscopy is the centroid of the
Br$\gamma$ line ($\lambda_{\rm rest} = 2.16612\,\mu$m). The model
wavelength ratio factorises exactly as
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda_{\rm obs}}{\lambda_{\rm emit}}
 = (1 + z_{\rm D})(1 + z_{\rm tD})(1 + z_{\rm grav}),
\label{eq:zfactor}
\end{equation}
where the three factors isolate the classical line-of-sight Doppler
term $z_{\rm D}$, the special-relativistic transverse (time-dilation)
term $z_{\rm tD} = \gamma - 1$, and the gravitational redshift
$z_{\rm grav}$ at the emission point (\texttt{redshift\_terms}
evaluates each along the cached trajectory). The quoted model radial
velocity is the \emph{apparent}
$v_r = c\,(\lambda_{\rm obs}/\lambda_{\rm rest} - 1)$ — the same
convention the observers use — so the relativistic terms appear as an
excess over a Keplerian orbit with identical elements, which is
precisely the quantity GRAVITY measured in 2018
(Section~\ref{sec:redshift}).

Fig.~\ref{fig:redshiftdecomp} stacks the two relativistic terms through
the 2018 pericentre passage: they are comparable in size (the
$2/(1+e)$ near-equality worked out in Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}), sum to
the $\sim 200$~km/s excess, and each is evaluated with no adjustable
amplitude — the fitted mass and geometry force both.
