\noindent This last primer chapter collects the working tools — units,

\section{Natural units: setting $G = c = 1$}

Physicists working with black holes almost always set the gravitational
constant $G$ and the speed of light $c$ equal to~1. This is not a trick
or an approximation; it is a choice of units, exactly like measuring
road distances in hours of driving. Once you do it, mass, length, and
time all become interchangeable, and a single symbol $M$ can serve as
the yardstick for everything: lengths are quoted in units of the
gravitational radius (``the ISCO is at $6\,M$''), times in units of the
light-crossing time of that radius. For \sgra{}, one $M$ of length is
about 0.042~au and one $M$ of time is about 21 seconds. The huge
practical benefit is universality: every formula and every rendered
image in Parts~II--III applies to \emph{any} black hole; only the final
conversion to kilometres, seconds, or microarcseconds depends on the
actual mass (that conversion is Chapter~\ref{ch:calib}).

\begin{worked}{One $M$, three ways}
For \sgra{}, the yardstick $M$ — the gravitational radius $Gm/c^2$ —
evaluates, in everyday units, to
\[
M = 4.297\times10^{6}\times1.48~{\rm km}
\approx 6.35\times10^{9}~{\rm m} = 0.0424~{\rm au},
\]
using the handy fact that one solar mass equals $1.48$~km of length. As
a \emph{time}, divide by $c$ once more:
$6.35\times10^{9}/3.00\times10^{8} = 21.2$~seconds — the natural tick
of \sgra{} (one orbit at the innermost stable circle takes about two
dozen ticks, some eight and a half minutes). As an \emph{angle} seen
from Earth, divide instead by the distance,
$8\,277~{\rm pc} = 2.554\times10^{20}$~m, giving
$2.49\times10^{-11}$~radians; radians convert to microarcseconds via
$\times\,2.063\times10^{11}$, so one $M$ subtends
$5.12~\muas$ — the $\UasPerM~\muas$ per $M$ used throughout
Chapter~\ref{ch:calib}. Mass as kilometres, seconds, and
microarcseconds: one number, three costumes.
\end{worked}

A related habit: Part~II speaks of \emph{vectors} (arrows with direction
and length — a velocity is one) and occasionally \emph{tensors}. For
reading this report it is enough to know that a tensor is a
machine that eats vectors and returns numbers, in a way that all
observers agree on. The metric of Chapter~\ref{ch:f2} is the only tensor
we ever really need: it eats two little coordinate steps and returns
their true separation.

\section{Error bars, and the meaning of $\chi^2$}

Every real measurement comes with an uncertainty — an \emph{error bar} —
and the whole craft of fitting models to data is bookkeeping those error
bars honestly. The universal bookkeeping device is called
\emph{chi-squared} ($\chi^2$), and it answers one question: \emph{by how
many error bars, in total, does the model miss the data?}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dgchi}). For each data point, take the miss, divide by
that point's error bar, square it (so misses in either direction count
alike, and big misses count a lot), and add up over all points.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/dg_chi2.pdf}
\caption{$\chi^2$ made visible. Two candidate models through the same
data: the good one misses each point by about one error bar
($\chi^2 \approx$ number of points); the wrong one racks up an enormous
total. Fitting means turning model knobs until $\chi^2$ is as small as
it gets.}
\label{fig:dgchi}
\end{figure}

Two rules of thumb make $\chi^2$ readable. A model that is right, fitted
to honest error bars, should miss each point by about one error bar on
average — so $\chi^2$ divided by the number of data points (the
\emph{reduced} $\chi^2$) should be near 1. Much bigger means the model
(or the error bars) are wrong; much smaller means the error bars were
pessimistic. Our headline S2 fit lands at $\chi^2_\nu = \FitChiRed$
(Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}) with the published error bars used exactly
as given.

And once the best knob-settings are found, \emph{how well known} is each
knob? Wiggle a knob away from its best value and $\chi^2$ climbs; the
range over which it climbs by less than a set amount is the knob's
\emph{confidence interval} — the ``$\pm$'' quoted after every fitted
number in this report. When we write $M = \FitMass \pm \FitMassErr$
million suns, the $\pm$ is precisely such an interval.

\begin{worked}{A five-point $\chi^2$, by hand}
Toy data: five measurements with error bars, and a candidate model
curve. Suppose the model misses the five points by
$0.8,\,-1.2,\,0.3,\,1.5,\,-0.6$ error bars respectively. Then
\[
\chi^2 = 0.64+1.44+0.09+2.25+0.36 = 4.8,
\]
about one per point ($\chi^2_\nu \approx 1$): a healthy fit. Now slide
the whole curve up by two error bars. The misses become
$2.8,\,0.8,\,2.3,\,3.5,\,1.4$, and
\[
\chi^2 = 7.84+0.64+5.29+12.25+1.96 = 28
\]
— nearly six per point. No committee is needed to reject the second
model; the ``ruling'' is nothing more than this arithmetic done
honestly. The real fits of Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting} do exactly the
same sum, with hundreds of measurements and thirteen knobs.
\end{worked}

\section{MCMC: mapping uncertainty by wandering}

For a model with thirteen knobs, the surface of $\chi^2$ over all knob
combinations is a thirteen-dimensional landscape, and simple wiggling
can miss how knobs trade off against one another. The heavy machinery
for this is \emph{Markov chain Monte Carlo} (MCMC): release a crowd of
random walkers into the landscape, with rules that make them drift
toward — and then wander within — the low-$\chi^2$ valleys
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dgmcmc}). After enough steps, \emph{where the walkers
spend their time is itself the answer}: dense regions are probable
parameter values, sparse regions improbable. Histograms of the walkers'
positions give every parameter's uncertainty and every pairwise
trade-off at once. Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting} runs exactly this (32
walkers, thousands of steps, the full relativistic model evaluated at
every step) and checks that the walker map agrees with the simpler
wiggle estimate.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figures/dg_mcmc_walkers.pdf}
\caption{Three MCMC walkers released onto a two-parameter landscape
(grey contours: the low-$\chi^2$ valley). After an initial wander-in,
the walkers trace out the valley; their footprints map the uncertainty,
including the diagonal trade-off between the two parameters.}
\label{fig:dgmcmc}
\end{figure}

\section{The no-hair theorem in one picture}

One theorem gives this project its sharpest question. General relativity
predicts that a black hole, once settled, is the simplest macroscopic
object in nature: its exterior gravity is \emph{completely} fixed by two
numbers, mass and spin. All other detail of whatever fell in — its
shape, its chemistry, its history — is radiated away or swallowed. The
theorem's nickname says it: \emph{black holes have no hair}.

This is falsifiable. Any rotating body has a \emph{quadrupole moment} —
a measure of how squashed its gravity field is (Fig.~\ref{fig:dgquad}).
For planets and stars the squashing is free to vary; for a Kerr black
hole it is locked rigidly to the mass and spin. So the test is: measure
mass, spin, and quadrupole \emph{independently}, and check the lock. We
implement this by adding one knob to the spacetime — a quadrupole
surplus called $\epsq$, zero for a true Kerr hole — and asking the S2
data how far from zero it could be
(Chapter~\ref{ch:nohair}). The honest answer today: the data are fully
consistent with zero, with room to spare (a single star at 1\,500
horizon radii barely feels the quadrupole), and the same machinery is
ready for the closer stars that future instruments will find.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/dg_quadrupole.pdf}
\caption{The no-hair test in one picture. Left: for a Kerr black hole
the field's squashing (quadrupole) is fixed by mass and spin — nothing
to choose. Right: if the measured squashing deviated ($\epsq \neq 0$),
stellar orbits would precess differently, and Kerr would be falsified.}
\label{fig:dgquad}
\end{figure}

\begin{termsbox}
\item[Geometric units ($G=c=1$)] Measuring everything in units of the
black hole's own scale $M$; for \sgra{}, $1\,M \approx 0.042$~au
$\approx 21$~s.
\item[Tensor] A machine that eats vectors and returns
observer-independent numbers; the metric is the one that matters here.
\item[$\chi^2$ / reduced $\chi^2$] Total squared misses in units of the
error bars; near 1 per data point means a healthy fit.
\item[Confidence interval] The range over which a parameter can move
before $\chi^2$ climbs unacceptably; the ``$\pm$'' on every number.
\item[MCMC] Mapping parameter uncertainty by letting random walkers
populate the low-$\chi^2$ valleys.
\item[No-hair theorem] A black hole's exterior gravity is fixed by mass
and spin alone; tested here through the quadrupole knob $\epsq$.
\end{termsbox}
