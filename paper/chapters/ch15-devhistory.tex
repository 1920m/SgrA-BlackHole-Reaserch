\chapter{Development History, Iterations, and the Test Suite}
\label{ch:devhistory}

An instrument is only as credible as its validation record. This
chapter documents how the simulator was built — twelve gated phases, no
phase entered while any earlier gate was red — catalogues the complete
automated test suite with its exact tolerances, and retells the six
significant bug hunts as methods lessons, because what a validation
system \emph{caught} is the best evidence for what it would catch.

\section{Gated development}
\label{sec:gates}

Development proceeded through the gates of Table~\ref{tab:gates}; each
passing gate was tagged in version control, and every criterion is
asserted by code — a Catch2 test, a JSON metrology artefact, or a
validation-layer counter — never by eye. The ordering embodies the
project's central discipline: \emph{no GPU work until the physics was
proven on the CPU} (Gates 0--5 are pure CPU and science), and no
calibration claims until the renderer had survived pixel-level
cross-validation (Gate 7).

Read as a sequence, the ladder tells the project's story in
miniature. Gates 0--2 built \emph{trust in the mathematics}: no
pixel existed yet, only conserved quantities and analytic boundary
comparisons — the phase where a sign error costs an afternoon instead
of a month. Gates 3--5 converted trusted mathematics into
\emph{science claims}: the first quantitative beaming ratio, the
first fit against real published data, the first constraint with an
error bar. Gates 6--9 were \emph{engineering under guard}: the GPU
was allowed to render only what the CPU had already proven
(the pixel-difference gate is the enforcement mechanism), and the
interactive shell was allowed to exist only with validation layers
permanently on. Gates 10--12 \emph{closed the loop} back to the sky:
calibration against EHT, and the reproducibility contract that turned
a working simulator into a defensible report. The ordering was not
aesthetic: on three occasions (the tolerance-dependent shadow, the
Keplerian period error, the covariance failure — all below) a gate
refused to pass, and in each case the failure was caught at the
cheapest possible layer because nothing downstream had been built on
it yet.

Three design habits are visible in the table and are recommended
practice for any numerical-relativity-adjacent codebase. \emph{Limits
before features}: every Kerr formula is pinned at $\astar = 0$ against
Schwarzschild before it is used at spin. \emph{Two implementations
where possible}: the Carter/Mino and Christoffel integrators cross-check
each other on identical rays, so a bug must strike both formulations
identically to pass. \emph{Analytic anchors over regression values}:
wherever a closed form exists (Synge, Bardeen, BPT, the Page--Thorne
expansion, the quadrupole precession), the test asserts against it —
regression-only tests would happily fossilise a wrong constant.

\section{Bug hunts as methods lessons}
\label{sec:bughunts}

\subsection{The tolerance-dependent shadow}
The Phase-1 boundary metrology initially returned Schwarzschild shadow
radii that \emph{moved} as the integrator tolerance was changed —
by parts in $10^3$, far above the bisection depth. The hidden
\texttt{[.probe]} diagnostic was written to map the failure: rays in a
narrow bracket around the critical angle, classified across tolerances,
flipped from captured to escaped wholesale. The error-budget analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:projection} identified the mechanism — benign
per-step drift amplified by the long flat approach leg against a small
discrimination feature — and per-step constraint projection removed it,
making the boundary tolerance-independent at the $10^{-11}$ level
(Fig.~\ref{fig:projboundary}). \emph{Lesson: for separatrix
observables, budget accumulated constraint drift against the
discrimination scale, not against the state itself.}

\subsection{The quadrupole sign}
The first quasi-Kerr implementation produced an $\epsq$ pericentre
shift with the correct magnitude and the \emph{wrong sign} — invisible
to any single test, because both the analytic formula and the numeric
orbit could have carried the flip. The fix was pinned by a
\emph{two-sided} test pair: the analytic
$\delta\omega_Q = -3\pi\epsq(M/p)^2$ evaluated independently from the
Newtonian $P_2$ perturbation, against the measured apsidal advance of
integrated orbits at both signs of $\epsq$. \emph{Lesson: signs need
two independent derivations; one implementation testing itself proves
consistency, not correctness.}

\subsection{Keplerian initial conditions and the 1.6 per cent period}
Phase-4 fits initially refused to reproduce the published S2 period at
its quoted precision. The cause was the initial-condition shortcut —
launching at pericentre with the Newtonian vis-viva speed — which
mis-states the relativistic period by 1.6 per cent at S2's eccentricity
(Section~\ref{sec:darwin}), two orders above the measurement error. The
Darwin turning-point construction replaced it; the Kepler-third-law
test now pins the relation. \emph{Lesson: an approximation harmless for
rendering can be fatal for fitting; error budgets are per-use-case.}

\subsection{The vanishing mass uncertainty}
The library covariance reported $\sigma_M$ consistent with zero — a
fitted mass ``known'' to nine digits from 145 imaging epochs, which is
absurd on its face. Section~\ref{sec:svdfail} dissects the cause: SVD
truncation of the near-degenerate $M$--$R_0$ direction. The explicit
central-difference $(J^{\mathsf T}J)^{-1}$ replaced it, and the MCMC
posterior later confirmed the corrected ellipse end-to-end.
\emph{Lesson: covariance regularisation silently converts ``weakly
constrained'' into ``perfectly known''; check the singular spectrum
before trusting any library covariance.}

\subsection{The brick-red disk}
The live renderer's outer disk rendered as a flat brick-coloured
annulus. Not a physics bug: the Page--Thorne brightness spans
$\sim r^{-3}$, the display window compressed 24 stops into an LUT whose
lower quarter was nearly one colour, and everything beyond $\sim 10\,M$
quantised to it. The fix was purely in the display layer — LUT ramps
graded over the full domain and a calibrated log window — with the
physics untouched, verified by the unchanged \texttt{ctest} suite.
\emph{Lesson: keep display grading and physics in separable layers, so
a cosmetic failure cannot be ``fixed'' in the equations.}

\subsection{The turbulence seam}
The display-only turbulence layer (Chapter~\ref{ch:instrument})
initially showed a visible seam at $\phi = 0$: value noise sampled on a
non-periodic domain. Replaced with azimuthally periodic noise; a
regression eye-check at four azimuths entered the render checklist.
\emph{Lesson: any function of an angle must be built periodic, not
wrapped after the fact.}
