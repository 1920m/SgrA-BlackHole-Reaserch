\chapter{Light, Heat, and Colour: Reading the Disk}
\label{ch:f4}

\section{Hot things glow, and colour is a thermometer}

Heat a poker in a fire and it glows dull red; hotter, and it turns
orange, then yellow, then blue-white. This everyday sequence is one of
the most useful facts in astrophysics: a dense hot body glows with a
spectrum — a recipe of colours — that depends on its temperature and on
almost nothing else. Physicists call this ideal glow \emph{blackbody
radiation}, and the temperature-to-colour dictionary is exact: around
3\,000 degrees looks ember-orange, 6\,000 degrees looks white like the
Sun's surface, 20\,000 degrees looks blue-white. The renders in this
report use precisely this dictionary — every pixel's colour comes from a
computed temperature pushed through the same colour-matching tables that
describe human vision (Chapter~\ref{ch:disk}), not from an artist's
palette.

\section{The accretion disk}

Gas falling toward a black hole almost never falls straight in. Like
water circling a drain, it has some rotation, so it settles into a
flattened, swirling \emph{accretion disk}. Within the disk, gas at each
radius orbits at its own rate — inner gas circles faster than outer gas,
just as Mercury outpaces Neptune — and the resulting internal friction
does two things: it lets the gas drift gradually inward, and it heats
the disk white-hot. The energy released is prodigious. Matter spiralling
through a disk onto a rapidly spinning black hole can radiate away tens
of per cent of its entire rest-mass energy — compared with less than one
per cent for nuclear fusion. Our simulator computes this efficiency
exactly (it is the $\eta$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:kerr}): about
\EtaSchw{} per cent for a non-spinning hole, rising to
\EtaSgrA{} per cent at \sgra{}'s spin, because the disk's inner edge (the
ISCO of Chapter~\ref{ch:f3}) reaches so much deeper into the well.

The model disk used throughout Part~III is the classic
\emph{Novikov--Thorne} thin disk: geometrically thin like a vinyl record,
hottest just outside the ISCO, cooling outward. It is an idealisation —
the real flow around \sgra{} is a hot, puffed-up, far dimmer affair — and
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} is careful about which conclusions survive
that difference. The disk model gives our images their brightness and
colour; crucially, the \emph{geometry} of shadow and photon ring comes
from the spacetime itself, not from the disk details.

\section{Why one side of the disk blazes}

Every published image of a black-hole disk — the EHT rings,
\emph{Interstellar}'s Gargantua, the renders in this report — is
conspicuously brighter on one side. That asymmetry is a speed effect,
not a lighting effect.

The inner disk orbits at an appreciable fraction of the speed of light.
When a source of light moves toward you that fast, three effects
conspire: its light is squeezed forward like spray from a moving hose
(\emph{beaming}), arrives blueshifted, and arrives at a faster clip. The
approaching side of the disk therefore appears far brighter — in our
reference render at $\astar = 0.94$, viewed nearly edge-on, the
approaching limb outshines the receding one by a factor of
\BeamRatio{} in total light (Chapter~\ref{ch:disk}). The receding side is
correspondingly dimmed and reddened. The whole effect is packaged in a
single number per light ray, the \emph{redshift factor} $g$: the ratio of
the light's frequency as received to its frequency as emitted. Brightness
scales as $g^4$ — the fourth power — which is why the asymmetry is so
dramatic. If a simulated disk ever looks symmetric, its $g^4$ is wrong;
this was one of our hard validation gates
(Chapter~\ref{ch:devhistory}).

\section{The three redshifts}

That factor $g$ mixes three physically distinct effects, and pulling them
apart is exactly what the S2 measurements of Part~III require
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dgthreez}).

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/dg_three_redshifts.pdf}
\caption{The three ways a wavelength gets shifted. Classical Doppler:
motion along the line of sight stretches or compresses the wave train.
Transverse Doppler: a fast clock ticks slowly, so even sideways motion
reddens the light — a purely relativistic effect. Gravitational: light
climbing out of a gravity well arrives stretched.}
\label{fig:dgthreez}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Classical Doppler}: the familiar ambulance-siren shift.
Motion away from you stretches the waves (redshift); motion toward you
compresses them (blueshift). This is by far the largest term for an
orbiting star, and Newton would have predicted it too.
\item \emph{Transverse Doppler}: special relativity's addition. A fast
mover's clock runs slow, so its emitted waves come out at a reduced
rate — a redshift that appears even when the motion is purely sideways.
It scales with speed squared, so it only matters when things get truly
fast.
\item \emph{Gravitational redshift}: general relativity's addition,
from Chapter~\ref{ch:f2} — light climbing out of a gravitational well is
stretched. It scales with depth in the well.
\end{enumerate}

For the star S2 at its 2018 closest approach, the first term is several
thousand km/s, while the second and third are each worth only about a
hundred km/s — and, by a neat algebraic coincidence worked out in
Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}, almost exactly equal to each other. The
GRAVITY collaboration's 2018 detection of that little two-hundred-km/s
surplus over the Newtonian prediction was the first direct test of
general relativity beside a supermassive black hole. Reproducing it,
with no tunable knob, is one of this report's headline results: our
model peaks at \GrExcessPeak{}~km/s at epoch \GrExcessEpoch{}
(Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}).

\begin{termsbox}
\item[Blackbody radiation] The temperature-determined glow of a dense
hot body; the exact temperature-to-colour dictionary used in our
renders.
\item[Accretion disk] The flattened swirl of hot gas spiralling into a
black hole; modelled here as the Novikov--Thorne thin disk.
\item[Accretion efficiency ($\eta$)] The fraction of rest-mass energy
radiated by matter spiralling in: \EtaSchw{}\% (no spin) to
\EtaSgrA{}\% (\sgra{}'s spin).
\item[Doppler beaming] The forward-throwing of light by a fast-moving
source; makes the approaching disk limb $\sim$\BeamRatio$\times$
brighter in our reference render.
\item[Redshift factor $g$] Received frequency over emitted frequency
for one light ray; observed brightness scales as $g^4$.
\item[Classical / transverse / gravitational redshift] The three
distinct wavelength shifts; separating them at S2's pericentre is the
GRAVITY 2018 test.
\end{termsbox}
