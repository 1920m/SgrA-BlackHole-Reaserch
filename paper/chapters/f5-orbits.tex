\chapter{Orbits, S-Stars, and Weighing a Black Hole}
\label{ch:f5}

\section{Kepler's ellipse}

Four hundred years before anyone imagined black holes, Johannes Kepler
discovered that planets travel on \emph{ellipses} — squashed circles —
with the Sun at one \emph{focus}, a point offset from the centre
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dgkepler}). Three of his terms are used on nearly every
page of Part~III:

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/dg_kepler_ellipse.pdf}
\caption{The anatomy of a Kepler orbit. The attracting mass sits at one
focus; eccentricity measures how off-centre it is. The dots mark equal
time steps: the orbiting body loiters near apocentre and races through
pericentre.}
\label{fig:dgkepler}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{semi-major axis} $a$ — half the long diameter, the
orbit's overall size;
\item the \emph{eccentricity} $e$ — how squashed and off-centre the
ellipse is, from $0$ (circle) toward $1$ (a stretched sliver);
\item the \emph{pericentre} and \emph{apocentre} — the points of
closest and farthest approach. (For orbits around stars you may know
these as perihelion and aphelion.)
\end{itemize}

Kepler's second discovery is visible in the dots of
Fig.~\ref{fig:dgkepler}: bodies move \emph{fast near pericentre and slow
near apocentre}. His third links the orbit's size and period to the
central mass — bigger orbits take longer, and, decisively for us,
\emph{at fixed size, a heavier central mass means a faster orbit}.
Measure the size and the period of any orbit and the central mass falls
out. This is how we weigh everything in astronomy, from planets to
galaxies — and it is exactly how \sgra{} is weighed.

\section{The S-stars, and S2 above all}

Buried in the central light-month of the Galaxy is a swarm of ordinary
young stars on extraordinary orbits — the \emph{S-stars}. Since 1992,
two teams (in Garching and in Los Angeles) have photographed the region
every year, watching individual stars trace out their ellipses around
an invisible focus. The star \emph{S2} is the jewel of the collection:
bright enough to measure precisely, with a period of just 16 years —
short enough that we have now watched it complete more than one and a
half full loops. Its orbit is extreme by any planetary standard:
eccentricity 0.88, pericentre speed near 7\,700~km/s (2.6\,\% of the
speed of light), diving to about 1\,500 horizon radii from the black
hole.

This report fits the complete published S2 data set — 145 positions and
44 velocities spanning 1992--2016 (Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}) — plus four
more S-stars in a joint fit (Chapter~\ref{ch:cluster}).

\begin{worked}{How fast is S2 at pericentre?}
Newton's toolkit — accurate to about a per cent even this close to a
black hole — gives the speed anywhere on a Kepler orbit through the
\emph{vis-viva} relation, $v^2 = Gm\,(2/r - 1/a)$. There is a
convenient set of units: with $r$ and $a$ in au and $v$ in km/s, $Gm$
for one solar mass is $887$ (because the Earth, at $r=a=1$~au, orbits
at $29.8$~km/s). For \sgra{}, multiply by $4.27$ million. S2's orbit
has $a \approx 1\,033$~au and eccentricity $e = 0.883$, putting
pericentre at $r_p = a(1-e) = 121$~au:
\[
v_p^2 = 4.27\times10^{6}\times887\times
\left(\tfrac{2}{121}-\tfrac{1}{1033}\right)
= 5.9\times10^{7}~{\rm (km/s)^2},
\]
so $v_p \approx 7\,700$~km/s — the 2.6\% of light speed quoted above.
The same formula at apocentre ($r_a = a(1+e) = 1\,944$~au) gives just
$480$~km/s: sixteen times slower. That factor is Kepler's second law
made vivid, and it is why the dots of Fig.~\ref{fig:dgkepler} bunch up
at the far end of the ellipse.
\end{worked}

\section{Two channels: positions and speeds}

Everything we know about the S-stars comes through two complementary
kinds of measurement (Fig.~\ref{fig:dgastro}).

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/dg_astrometry_rv.pdf}
\caption{The two observation channels. Left — astrometry: the star's
position on the sky, measured year after year in milliarcseconds,
traces the projected ellipse. Right — spectroscopy: the wavelength of a
known line in the star's light shifts with its line-of-sight speed
(the Doppler effect of Chapter~\ref{ch:f4}).}
\label{fig:dgastro}
\end{figure}

\emph{Astrometry} is simply measuring where the star is on the sky, epoch
by epoch — in milliarcseconds, using adaptive-optics cameras on the
world's largest telescopes and, since 2017, the GRAVITY interferometer,
which links four 8-metre telescopes into one virtual 130-metre one.
Astrometry sees the orbit \emph{projected} on the sky, like the shadow
of a hoop tilted against a wall.

\emph{Spectroscopy} measures how fast the star moves \emph{along} the
line of sight, via the Doppler shift of a hydrogen absorption line in
its spectrum (the Br$\gamma$ line, in the infrared where the Galactic
dust is transparent). This ``radial velocity'' channel sees exactly the
component of motion that astrometry cannot.

Neither channel alone determines everything; together they overdetermine
the orbit. And one beautiful consequence deserves its own sentence:
because astrometry gives the orbit's \emph{angular} size while the
velocities give its \emph{physical} scale, combining them yields the
\emph{distance to the Galactic centre} — a geometric distance
measurement across 26\,000 light-years with no cosmic distance ladder
involved. Our fit recovers both the mass and this distance
(Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}).

\begin{worked}{Weighing \sgra{} in three lines}
The fitted S2 orbit of Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting} has angular semi-major
axis $\FitSma$~milliarcseconds, period $\FitPeriod$~years, and the
distance comes out at $\FitDist$~parsecs. Kepler's third law then
weighs the black hole with startling economy. First convert the angle
to a physical length — this is what the parsec is \emph{for}: one au
subtends exactly one arcsecond at one parsec, so
\[
a = 0.12482'' \times 8\,272~{\rm pc} = 1\,033~{\rm au}.
\]
Second, apply Kepler's third law in solar units (mass $= a^3/P^2$, with
$a$ in au, $P$ in years, mass in Suns):
\[
m = \frac{(1\,033)^3}{(16.06)^2}
= \frac{1.10\times10^{9}}{258} \approx 4.27\ \hbox{million solar masses}.
\]
Three lines of arithmetic land within a fraction of a per cent of the
full relativistic fit, $\FitMass\pm\FitMassErr$ million
(Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}) — because S2 spends most of its 16 years in
territory where Newton is an excellent approximation. Everything beyond
these three lines — precession, redshift excess, the no-hair test —
lives in the small, precious relativistic corrections.
\end{worked}

\section{Einstein's fingerprints on the orbit}

If gravity were exactly Newtonian, S2's ellipse would repeat itself
forever, fixed in space. General relativity disagrees in two measured
ways.

First, the \emph{redshift excess} of Chapter~\ref{ch:f4}: at the 2018
pericentre passage, S2's spectral lines arrived about 200~km/s redder
than any Newtonian orbit could explain — time dilation plus
gravitational redshift, detected by GRAVITY in 2018.

Second, \emph{orbital precession}: the ellipse itself slowly pivots. Each
orbit, the pericentre direction advances by a small angle, so the path
traces a rosette rather than a closed ring (Fig.~\ref{fig:dgrosette}).
For Mercury this famous advance is 43 arcseconds per century and was the
first triumph of general relativity; for S2 it is about 12
\emph{arcminutes} per 16-year orbit — some fifty thousand times faster —
and GRAVITY confirmed it in 2020. Our simulator reproduces both effects
with no adjustable amplitudes: the fitted mass and geometry force them
(Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}).

\begin{worked}{The rosette angle}
The pericentre advance per orbit has a closed form, derived in
Chapter~\ref{ch:sstar}:
\[
\Delta\varpi = \frac{6\pi\,Gm}{c^2\,a\,(1-e^2)}.
\]
Every ingredient is already on the table. The mass-as-length:
$Gm/c^2 = 4.27\times10^{6}\times1.48~{\rm km} = 0.0421$~au. The
denominator: $a(1-e^2) = 1\,033\times(1-0.883^2) = 228$~au. So
\[
\Delta\varpi = \frac{6\pi\times0.0421}{228} = 3.5\times10^{-3}\
\hbox{radians} = 0.20^\circ \approx 12\ \hbox{arcminutes per orbit},
\]
the very advance GRAVITY confirmed in 2020 (the full model gives
$\PrecSchw'$; Chapter~\ref{ch:fitting}). Mercury's celebrated 43
arcseconds per \emph{century} is this same formula fed with one solar
mass and Mercury's orbit — S2's advance per unit time is some fifty
thousand times larger, which is what makes a sixteen-year experiment
possible in a human career.
\end{worked}

\begin{termsbox}
\item[Ellipse, focus, semi-major axis, eccentricity] The shape language
of orbits (Fig.~\ref{fig:dgkepler}).
\item[Pericentre / apocentre] Closest and farthest points of an orbit;
S2's pericentre is $\sim$120~au at 2.6\% of light speed.
\item[S-stars, S2] The stars orbiting \sgra{}; S2, with its 16-year
period, is the most precisely measured.
\item[Astrometry] Measuring sky positions over time (milliarcseconds);
gives the projected orbit.
\item[Radial velocity (spectroscopy)] Measuring line-of-sight speed from
Doppler-shifted spectral lines.
\item[Geometric distance] Combining both channels yields the distance to
the Galactic centre directly.
\item[Precession / rosette] The slow pivot of the orbital ellipse
predicted by GR; $12'$ per orbit for S2, confirmed 2020.
\end{termsbox}
