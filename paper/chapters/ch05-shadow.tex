\chapter{The Reference Ray Tracer, Shadow, and Critical Curve}
\label{ch:shadow}

The CPU reference tracer is the project's ground truth: every downstream
artefact — disk images, sub-ring metrology, the GPU port — is validated
against it, and it in turn is validated against closed-form theory. This
chapter constructs the camera (\texttt{zamo\_tetrad},
\texttt{camera\_ray} in \texttt{physics/src/raytrace\_cpu.cpp}), derives
the two analytic anchors (Synge's shadow angle and Bardeen's critical
curve), presents the boundary metrology
(\texttt{shadow\_boundary\_angle}), and develops the spherical photon
orbits and their Lyapunov exponents, which quantify the photon-ring
substructure of Chapter~\ref{ch:ring}.

\section{The ZAMO camera and ray launching}
\label{sec:camera}

A camera in curved spacetime needs a local orthonormal frame — a tetrad
— to translate ``pixel direction'' into a four-momentum. The natural
choice at BL position $(r_{\rm cam}, \theta_{\rm cam})$ is the
zero-angular-momentum observer of Section~\ref{sec:zamo}: four-velocity
\begin{equation}
u^\mu = u^t\,(1,\ 0,\ 0,\ \omega), \qquad
\omega = -\frac{g_{t\phi}}{g_{\phi\phi}}, \qquad
u^t = \bigl[-(g_{tt} + 2\omega g_{t\phi} +
\omega^2 g_{\phi\phi})\bigr]^{-1/2},
\label{eq:zamofourvel}
\end{equation}
completed to an orthonormal basis by the unit vectors along $r$,
$\theta$, and the remaining direction in the $t$--$\phi$ plane
(\texttt{zamo\_tetrad}). Written out, the four legs are
\begin{equation}
e_{\hat 0} = \frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\partial_t + \omega\,
\partial_\phi\right),
\quad
e_{\hat r} = \sqrt{\frac{\Delta}{\Sigma}}\,\partial_r,
\quad
e_{\hat\theta} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\Sigma}}\,\partial_\theta,
\quad
e_{\hat\phi} = \sqrt{\frac{\Sigma}{A}}\,
\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\,\partial_\phi,
\label{eq:zamotetrad}
\end{equation}
with the lapse $\alpha = \sqrt{\Delta\Sigma/A}$ of
Section~\ref{sec:zamo}; orthonormality is a two-line check using the
determinant identity \eqref{eq:blockdet} — for instance
$g(e_{\hat 0}, e_{\hat 0}) = (g_{tt} + 2\omega g_{t\phi} + \omega^2
g_{\phi\phi})/\alpha^2 = (g_{tt}g_{\phi\phi} -
g_{t\phi}^2)/(g_{\phi\phi}\,\alpha^2) = -1$. This observer exists
everywhere outside the
horizon — including inside the ergosphere, where a static camera would
be impossible — and reduces to the static observer at $a = 0$, so a
single camera implementation serves every spin. The conserved
quantities of a launched photon follow by contraction: a locally
measured direction with celestial angles maps to
$E = \alpha + \omega L_z$ scalings and $L_z \propto
\sin\theta\sqrt{A/\Sigma}$ factors, which is how frame dragging enters
the optics — two photons launched at mirror-image local angles carry
\emph{different} $L_z/E$ once $\omega \neq 0$, and everything
asymmetric about Kerr images descends from that line.

A pixel at normalised image-plane position $(x, y)$ defines a unit
direction $(n^{\hat r}, n^{\hat\theta}, n^{\hat\phi})$ on the local sky;
contracting with the tetrad legs and fixing the local photon energy to
unity gives the BL four-momentum $k^\mu$, from which
\texttt{make\_null\_ray} extracts $(E, L_z, Q)$ algebraically
(Section~\ref{sec:makenullray}). Rays are launched \emph{forward}: null
geodesics are time-reversible, so where a launched ray lands is what the
pixel sees, and integrating outward avoids sampling subtleties at the
camera. The production camera sits at $r_{\rm cam} = 1000\,M$ — far
enough that the residual bending between camera and infinity is a
correction below $10^{-3}$, applied analytically in
Section~\ref{sec:metrology}.

\section{Grid-free boundary metrology}
\label{sec:metrology}

Measuring a shadow from a rendered image is limited by the pixel pitch.
The tracer instead measures the boundary directly in camera-angle space:
along each image-plane direction $\psi$, the capture/escape transition
is bracketed and bisected (\texttt{shadow\_boundary\_angle}), each probe
being a full geodesic integration with the projection of
Chapter~\ref{ch:numerics}. Sixty bisection steps localise the critical
angle $\alpha(\psi)$ to $\sim$$10^{-12}$~rad — no pixel grid is involved
anywhere, and thanks to the tolerance-independence established in
Fig.~\ref{fig:projboundary}, the result is a property of the spacetime,
not of the integrator.

Angular radii convert to impact parameters through
\begin{equation}
b = \frac{r_{\rm cam}\,\sin\alpha}{\sqrt{1 - 2M/r_{\rm cam}}},
\label{eq:btoalpha}
\end{equation}
the flat-space relation corrected for the gravitational bending of the
reference ``straight line'' at finite camera radius (the correction is
the $1/\sqrt{1-2M/r}$ factor; at $r_{\rm cam} = 10^3\,M$ it shifts $b$
by one part in $10^3$ and is exact for Schwarzschild). Diameters are
quoted in units of $M$; the conversion to microarcseconds on the real
sky is the calibration step of Chapter~\ref{ch:calib}.

\section{Schwarzschild validation: the Synge formula}
\label{sec:synge}

For $\astar = 0$ the whole boundary follows in three lines, first given
by \citet{Synge1966}. Equatorial photons experience the effective
potential $V(r) = (1 - 2M/r)/r^2$ (the $b$-independent factor of the
radial potential \eqref{eq:Req} at $a=0$): $V'(r) = 0$ gives the photon
sphere $r_{\rm ph} = 3M$; the critical impact parameter is
$b_c = 1/\sqrt{V(3M)} = \sqrt{27}\,M$; and an observer at finite
$r_{\rm obs}$ sees the shadow at angular radius
\begin{equation}
\sin\alpha_{\rm sh} = \frac{\sqrt{27}\,M\,\sqrt{1 - 2M/r_{\rm obs}}}
{r_{\rm obs}}
\label{eq:synge}
\end{equation}
(\texttt{schwarzschild\_shadow\_angle}). The traced boundary agrees with
\eqref{eq:synge} to a relative deviation of $5\times10^{-16}$ — machine
precision — and is circular to \SchwSpreadPct{} per cent over 32
directions, with an equivalent diameter of $\SchwDiamM\,M$ against the
analytic $2\sqrt{27}\,M \simeq 10.3923\,M$. The same anchor reappears
independently in the $(\astar, i)$ sweep of Chapter~\ref{ch:calib}:
every $\astar = 0$ grid row returns it identically at all inclinations,
as spherical symmetry demands.

The approach to $b_c$ carries its own universal law, worth stating
because Section~\ref{sec:spherical} re-derives it from a different
direction. Photons with $b = b_c(1 + \epsilon)$, $\epsilon \ll 1$,
linger near the photon sphere for a winding angle that grows as
$-\ln\epsilon$: the deflection diverges \emph{logarithmically} at the
critical impact parameter. Inverting, the impact-parameter window that
achieves $n$ additional half-windings shrinks as $e^{-\pi n}$ — each
successive image of the source sky is squeezed into an annulus
$e^{-\pi} \approx 0.043$ times thinner than the last, and a full extra
orbit costs $e^{-2\pi} \approx 1/535$. This is the demagnification
ladder first exhibited in \citet{Luminet1979}'s photograph of a
Schwarzschild disk, and the exponent is exactly the Lyapunov
$\gamma = \pi$ established below; the traced flux ratios of
Chapter~\ref{ch:ring} will land on it.

\section{The Bardeen critical curve}
\label{sec:bardeen}

For $a \neq 0$ the shadow boundary is the image of the \emph{photon
shell} — the family of unstable spherical photon orbits of
Section~\ref{sec:turningpoints}. On the shell the radial potential has a
double root, $R(\tilde r) = R'(\tilde r) = 0$; solving the pair for the
two conserved ratios $\xi = L_z/E$ and $\eta = Q/E^2$ as functions of
the shell radius $\tilde r$ is mechanical once set up: with the quartic
in the Bardeen form \eqref{eq:bardeenquartic}, the two conditions are
linear in the combinations $\xi^2 + \eta$ and $(\xi - a)^2 + \eta$, so
one solves the $2\times2$ linear system and then untangles $\xi$ from
the difference of the two combinations, which is linear in $\xi$. The
result, after the reduction of \citet{Bardeen1973},
\begin{align}
\xi(\tilde r) &= \frac{\tilde r^2(3M-\tilde r) - a^2(\tilde r+M)}
{a(\tilde r-M)},
&
\eta(\tilde r) &= \frac{\tilde r^3\bigl[4a^2M -
\tilde r(\tilde r-3M)^2\bigr]}{a^2(\tilde r-M)^2}.
\label{eq:bardeen}
\end{align}
For an observer at inclination $\theta_o$ the shell maps to the image
plane as
\begin{equation}
\alpha = -\frac{\xi}{\sin\theta_o}, \qquad
\beta = \pm\sqrt{\eta + a^2\cos^2\theta_o - \xi^2\cot^2\theta_o},
\label{eq:criticalimage}
\end{equation}
tracing the closed \emph{critical curve} as $\tilde r$ runs over the
interval where $\beta^2 \geq 0$ — from near $r_{\rm ph}^+$ (prograde
edge) to near $r_{\rm ph}^-$ (retrograde edge). Three limit checks pin
\eqref{eq:bardeen} to earlier chapters. At the shell edges $\eta = 0$:
the spherical orbits degenerate to the equatorial circular orbits, and
$\tilde r$ solves the photon cubic \eqref{eq:photoncubic}. As
$a \to 0$ the parametrisation degenerates to the single statement
$\xi^2 + \eta = 27M^2$ — every ``spherical orbit'' sits at $3M$ and
the critical curve is the Synge circle, which is why a circular traced
shadow at $\astar > 0$ would signal broken spin coupling (a failure
mode the test suite explicitly probes). And the zero of $\xi(\tilde r)$
marks the \emph{polar} spherical orbit — the one photon family that
crosses the spin axis — whose radius at $\astar = 0.94$ the
\texttt{sweep\_tool} family of Fig.~\ref{fig:sphericalorbits} contains
as its $\xi = 0$ member. For our actual \sgra{} viewing geometry,
$\theta_o \approx 30^\circ$ from the pole, the visible window of
$\tilde r$ narrows — an observer near the axis receives photons from
a sub-range of the shell centred on the polar orbit — and the critical
curve rounds toward a displaced near-circle: the geometric reason the
EHT cannot read the spin from the ring shape at \sgra{}'s inclination
(Chapter~\ref{ch:calib} quantifies this as the axis-ratio map). Fig.~\ref{fig:critcurves}
overlays the curves for four spins: growing spin flattens and displaces
the prograde side, exactly as frame dragging predicts, while the overall
scale barely changes — the geometric root of the spin--inclination
degeneracy quantified in Chapter~\ref{ch:calib}.

of the Synge check: together they validate the tetrad, the launcher, the
integrator, and the bisection logic end to end.

\section{Spherical photon orbits and Lyapunov exponents}
\label{sec:spherical}

Each point of the critical curve corresponds to a spherical photon orbit
at some shell radius $\tilde r$, and each such orbit is unstable: a
photon displaced by $\delta r$ obeys, in Mino time,
$\delta\ddot r = \tfrac12 R''(\tilde r)\,\delta r$, growing as
$\exp\bigl[\sqrt{\tfrac12 R''}\,\tau\bigr]$. The observationally
meaningful rate is the growth per \emph{polar half-oscillation} — the
interval between successive equatorial crossings, which is what
separates one photon sub-ring from the next
(Chapter~\ref{ch:ring}):
\begin{equation}
\gamma(\tilde r) = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}R''(\tilde r)}\;
\Delta\tau_\theta,
\qquad
\Delta\tau_\theta = \int_{\theta_-}^{\theta_+}
\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\sqrt{\Theta(\theta)}},
\label{eq:lyapunov}
\end{equation}
with $R'' = 8E^2\tilde r^2 + 4EP - 2K$ from
differentiating~\eqref{eq:rpotgen} twice and the polar period evaluated
by the substitution $u = \cos\theta$ (the integral reduces to a smooth
quadrature over one quadrant; \texttt{sweep\_tool photonorbits}). Each
extra half-orbit demagnifies the corresponding sub-ring image by
$e^{-\gamma}$ \citep{Johnson2020}.

Two exact properties emerge from the computed family
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sphericalorbits}). For Schwarzschild,
$\tfrac12 R''(3M) = 27E^2 = (\xi^2)E^2$ and
$\Delta\tau_\theta = \pi/\sqrt{\xi^2}$, so $\gamma = \pi$ for
\emph{every} orbit — the universal Schwarzschild value. More
surprisingly, the computed $\gamma$ returns to $\pi$ at the
\emph{equatorial edges} of the shell for every spin — the identity
$\tfrac12 R'' = (\xi^2 - a^2)E^2$ holds exactly at $\eta = 0$ — while
dipping in between: at $\astar = 0.94$ the polar orbit
($\xi = 0$) has $\gamma = \GammaPolarSgrA$, an $18$ per cent weaker
demagnification than $\pi$. This azimuth-dependence of the sub-ring
demagnification, spanning $[\GammaPolarSgrA, \pi]$ at the \sgra{} spin,
matches the range reported by \citet{Johnson2020} for M87*-like spins
and is the quantitative bridge between the shadow geometry of this
chapter and the flux ratios measured in Chapter~\ref{ch:ring}.

\section{Gravitational lensing of the disk and sky}
\label{sec:lensing}

The same machinery explains the signature look of every black-hole
image. Consider the disk of Chapter~\ref{ch:disk} viewed nearly edge-on.
Rays leaving the camera slightly \emph{above} the shadow pass over the
pole and are bent down onto the \emph{far side} of the disk, behind the
hole: the far side therefore appears \emph{above} the shadow, as a
luminous arch. Rays passing slightly below the shadow reach the far
side's \emph{underside}, painting a second, fainter arch below. Nothing
is behind anything at a black hole: the entire disk is visible at once,
wrapped around the silhouette — the geometry made famous by
\emph{Interstellar} \citep{James2015} and computed here by the same
backward tracing (Fig.~\ref{fig:trajectories} shows the ray families
responsible).

In the exactly-aligned limit the arches close into rings: a source
point diametrically behind the hole images to a full \emph{Einstein
ring}, and each additional winding number contributes a further ring
converging on the critical curve — the $n = 1, 2, \dots$ sub-rings of
Chapter~\ref{ch:ring}. On the spin axis this convergence produces a
bright polar caustic that appears in near-edge-on renders as a slim
vertical column of light above and below the hole; it is a
\emph{lensing} feature, not a jet, and is examined honestly in
Section~\ref{sec:axialtower}.
